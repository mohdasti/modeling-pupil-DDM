Abstract

This chapter presents a hierarchical Wiener diffusion decision model (DDM) for a response-signal change-detection task in older adults. The primary model maps task difficulty to drift rate (\(v\)) and boundary separation (\(a\)), with starting-point bias (\(z\)) varying by task and effort but constant across difficulty levels (reflecting the randomized trial design). Results showed that high-effort (40% MVC) conditions reduced drift rate (\(v\)) relative to low-effort (5% MVC) conditions, consistent with resource competition. Non-decision time (\(t_0\)) was modeled as constant across conditions (0.347 s) due to model stability constraints in the response-signal design. Contrary to predictions from adaptive caution theories, boundary separation (\(a\)) did not increase under high effort. Trial-wise pupil-linked arousal (TEPR) did not reliably modulate drift rate or starting-point bias when included as predictors. We report comprehensive quality assurance checks, manipulation checks independent of the DDM, model comparison via LOO cross-validation, and extensive posterior predictive checks with emphasis on subject-wise mid-body RT quantiles.

1 Introduction

1.1 Age-Related Slowing and Strategic Adaptation in Decision-Making

A hallmark of cognitive aging is a pervasive slowing of response times across virtually all task domains. Older adults consistently take longer to respond in speeded decision tasks than younger adults, a phenomenon historically interpreted as generalized slowing (Birren, 1965; Salthouse, 1985). Classic analyses using Brinley plots (Brinley, 1965) supported the idea of a global factor that uniformly slows processing with age (Cerella, 1985; Salthouse, 1996). In fact, increased age is associated with a decrease in the speed of many mental operations, as posited by the processing-speed theory of cognitive aging (Salthouse, 1996). According to this view, slower processing leaves less time for higher-order operations, effectively creating a bottleneck in available cognitive resources. However, simply viewing aging as a uniform “slower clock speed” obscures important nuances. Older adults often maintain accuracy rates comparable to younger adults by adjusting their decision-making strategy: they tend to respond more cautiously, emphasizing accuracy over speed. In other words, rather than being a passive consequence of a slower brain, longer response times in older people can reflect an active, strategic adaptation aimed at preserving accuracy in the face of cognitive and neural challenges. Empirical evidence for this adaptive strategy comes from speed–accuracy tradeoff studies showing that, when given the option, older adults favor slower, more accurate responding, whereas younger adults are willing to respond faster at the cost of more errors (Rabbitt, 1979; Ratcliff et al., 2004; Starns & Ratcliff, 2010). This age-related shift toward a “slow but sure” decision mode is often interpreted as an adaptive mechanism: older adults may consciously or unconsciously slow down to avoid mistakes, compensating for declines in sensory or cognitive processing by “buying” extra decision time (Mata et al., 2007; Ratcliff et al., 2006).

Notably, this cautious response style means that aging effects on decision performance are not uniform across all components of processing. Contemporary cognitive aging models distinguish between generalized slowing and component-specific changes in decision dynamics. For example, older adults experience sensory and perceptual degradations, often conceptualized as a reduction in neural signal-to-noise ratio (Li et al., 2001; Lindenberger & Baltes, 1994), as well as distinct motor slowing (Seidler et al., 2010), which each can prolong portions of the response time independent of decision strategy. Simultaneously, older adults exhibit changes in decision criteria, such as setting a higher threshold for required evidence before responding (Ratcliff et al., 2001, 2004, 2007; Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008). This raises an important question: does aging slow core cognitive processing speed, or do older adults simply recalibrate their speed–accuracy tradeoff by becoming more cautious? The answer appears to be a combination of both, depending on task demands. In relatively simple perceptual decision tasks—such as brightness discrimination or letter identification—healthy older adults often achieve accuracy similar to young adults by adopting conservative decision thresholds and taking more time, rather than by processing information more efficiently (Ratcliff et al., 2001, 2003). Critically, drift rates (the quality of evidence accumulation) are preserved in these simple perceptual tasks, indicating that fundamental cognitive processing capacity remains intact when task demands are low. In contrast, under more challenging conditions—specifically episodic memory tasks requiring recollection of contextual details (Spaniol et al., 2006) or complex visual searches requiring serial attention allocation (Madden & Allen, 1991)—older adults exhibit genuine declines in drift rates. This indicates real reductions in the quality of decision-driving information. Thus, while slower responses in simple tasks reflect a strategic emphasis on accuracy (criterion shift) and slower peripheral processes, complex tasks reveal a different pattern. When cognitive demands exceed the older brain’s capacity, seniors often cannot maintain the same quality of evidence accumulation as the young, leading to performance decrements when they attempt to respond quickly. These findings underscore that aging impacts decision-making through multiple pathways: a general tendency toward slower, more cautious responding, coupled with task-specific deficits in processing efficiency when cognitive demands are high.

1.2 Insights from Drift-Diffusion Modeling of Aging

To disentangle these component processes of decision-making, researchers increasingly turn to computational modeling approaches. One powerful framework is the Drift Diffusion Model (DDM), which provides a quantitative decomposition of choice reaction time data into psychologically interpretable parameters (Ratcliff, 1978; Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008; Voss et al., 2004). In a forced-choice decision task, the DDM conceptualizes the process as a gradual, noisy accumulation of evidence toward one of two decision boundaries (representing the response options). The key model parameters include:

  • Drift rate (\(v\)) – the average speed of evidence accumulation toward the correct decision. This reflects the quality or efficiency of information processing; a higher drift rate means the decision maker can extract and use task-relevant information more quickly.
  • Decision boundary (\(a\)) – the amount of evidence required to commit to a choice, often interpreted as response caution or threshold. A larger boundary separation indicates a more cautious strategy (waiting for more evidence before deciding), whereas a smaller boundary implies a more impulsive or speed-emphasizing strategy.
  • Starting point (\(z\)) – the initial bias or predisposition toward one option or the other before evidence is accumulated. If the starting point is centered (typically 0.5 in relative units), there is no pre-existing bias; deviations from center indicate an a priori bias to favor a particular response (e.g., a bias to say “yes” vs “no” in a detection task).
  • Non-decision time (\(t_0\)) – the duration of processes outside of the evidence accumulation itself, such as perceptual encoding of the stimulus and the motor execution of the response (Voss et al., 2004). Non-decision time accounts for aspects of the reaction time that are not decision-related.

Formally, the evidence accumulation process follows the stochastic differential equation (Ratcliff, 1978; Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008):

\[dX(t) = v \, dt + dW(t) \tag{1}\]

where \(X(t)\) is the accumulated evidence at time \(t\), \(v\) is the drift rate (evidence strength), and \(dW(t)\) is a Wiener noise process with unit variance. The process starts at \(X(0) = z \cdot a\), where \(z \in [0,1]\) is the starting-point bias (expressed as a proportion of boundary separation) and \(a > 0\) is the boundary separation (Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008). The decision process terminates when \(X(t)\) reaches either the upper boundary (\(X(t) = a\)) or the lower boundary (\(X(t) = 0\)). The total reaction time (RT) is the sum of the decision time and the non-decision time (\(t_0\)):

\[\text{RT} = t_{\text{decision}} + t_0 \tag{2}\]

By fitting the DDM to participants’ accuracy and response time distributions, one can infer how these latent parameters differ between groups (e.g. young vs. old) or conditions. This model-based approach has proved especially illuminating in aging research. Rather than relying on overall slowing measures alone, the DDM allows researchers to pinpoint which aspects of processing slow down or change with age and which remain intact.

The DDM mathematically formalizes the behavioral patterns described above. The model confirms that older adults’ slower responses are largely due to shifts in caution and peripheral processing, rather than uniformly impaired evidence accumulation. Specifically, older adults consistently exhibit significantly higher decision thresholds (\(a\)) than younger adults across a variety of tasks (Ratcliff et al., 2004; Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008), quantitatively capturing the strategic slowing: by widening the distance between boundaries, older adults counteract internal noise and maintain high accuracy. Additionally, the model isolates the contribution of peripheral slowing; older adults typically show increased non-decision times (\(t_0\)), often 80–100 ms longer than young adults (Ratcliff et al., 2004), reflecting age-related delays in motor execution and sensory encoding. Crucially, the DDM reveals that drift rate (\(v\))—the core measure of cognitive processing efficiency—is often remarkably preserved in aging for simple perceptual tasks (Ratcliff et al., 2001, 2003, 2004).

However, as noted previously, this preservation is not universal: in tasks taxing memory retrieval (Spaniol et al., 2006) or complex visual search (Madden & Allen, 1991), modeling confirms a decline in drift rates, indicating that for complex cognitive operations, the older brain does accumulate evidence more slowly. Furthermore, while older adults can adjust their boundaries, they often exhibit a rigidity in this setting; Starns and Ratcliff (2010) demonstrated that older adults fail to lower their boundaries (\(a\)) optimally under speed pressure, prioritizing accuracy even when the task incentivizes speed. Regarding bias (\(z\)), healthy aging is generally not associated with systematic shifts in starting point for simple tasks, though specific biases can emerge in memory paradigms (e.g., a conservative bias against “new” items to avoid false alarms) (Ratcliff et al., 2004; Spaniol et al., 2006). Overall, the application of drift-diffusion modeling provides a nuanced portrait of aging: it mathematically separates the strategic adaptations (increased \(a\)) and peripheral slowing (increased \(t_0\)) from the fundamental cognitive capacity (drift rate \(v\)), which remains intact in simple contexts but declines under high cognitive load.

Having established the baseline DDM profile of older adults, we turn to an important modulating factor: arousal and effort. In this chapter, we leverage the diffusion model to investigate how fluctuations in arousal (induced via physical effort) can alter these latent decision processes in older adults.

1.3 Arousal, Effort, and Decision Performance in Older Adults

Beyond baseline aging effects, cognitive performance is strongly influenced by the organism’s arousal state—the level of alertness or activation of physiological and neural systems. Classic theory, dating back to the Yerkes–Dodson law (Yerkes & Dodson, 1908), holds that the relationship between arousal and performance follows an inverted-U function: increasing arousal enhances performance up to an optimal point, after which further arousal (especially if reaching stress or anxiety levels) impairs performance. In the context of aging, this dynamic takes on special significance. Adaptive Gain Theory (AGT) (Aston-Jones & Cohen, 2005) provides a neural mechanism for this relationship, linking phasic and tonic Locus Coeruleus activity to optimal task performance. When this framework is extended to aging, researchers posit that the arousal–performance curve is altered, often manifesting as a leftward shift or compression of the inverted-U function (Mather & Harley, 2016; Mikneviciute et al., 2022). This implies that older adults may reach their “optimal” arousal peak at lower levels of objective demand than younger adults. Consequently, levels of effort or stress that might be engaging or beneficial for a younger adult (placing them at the peak of the curve) can push an older adult onto the “descending limb,” leading to supra-optimal arousal and performance decrements (Huang & Clewett, 2024; Mather & Harley, 2016).

Older adults typically have a reduced physiological capacity to sustain high arousal yet often need to exert greater mental effort to perform a given task at the same level as a younger person. Recent studies support the idea that effortful engagement is more taxing for older adults in measurable ways. For example, Hess and Ennis (Hess & Ennis, 2012) demonstrated that when older adults performed continuous arithmetic tasks (e.g., subtraction), they exhibited significantly larger increases in systolic blood pressure (SBP)—a physiological index of effortful arousal—than young adults, and this elevated physiological cost predicted greater fatigue on subsequent tasks. Furthermore, research directly relevant to the current paradigm has shown that concurrent physical effort can be detrimental to cognition in aging. Azer et al. (2023) found that while maintaining a concurrent moderate isometric handgrip, older adults showed significantly reduced accuracy in a visual working memory task with distractors, whereas younger adults remained unaffected. This supports the limited-capacity framework, suggesting that shared processing resources (Wickens, 2008) are more easily depleted in older adults, or that the combined demand drives arousal into a dysregulated state (Verhaeghen et al., 2003). However, it remains unclear how these effort- and arousal-related costs translate into the latent mechanisms of decision formation in older adults. Prior work has established that arousal and effort can alter observed performance (accuracy/RT) in aging, but has not directly tested which computational components of the decision process are affected (e.g., evidence quality vs. response caution vs. non-decision processes), particularly using a framework that jointly links physiological arousal (pupillometry) to diffusion-model parameters. The present chapter addresses this gap by combining pupillometry with hierarchical drift-diffusion modeling to identify how effort-induced arousal maps onto latent decision parameters in older adults.

What are the expected effects of arousal fluctuations on the decision-making mechanisms of older adults? By applying the drift-diffusion model (DDM), we can make specific hypotheses about how effort-induced arousal will modulate latent decision parameters. The present study manipulates arousal via physical effort (low vs. moderate), providing a controlled way to “push” older participants along the arousal curve. Behavioral theories such as Resource Competition and Limited Capacity predict that excessive effort will siphon processing resources away from the decision process, degrading evidence quality, whereas Adaptive Gain Theory predicts that older adults will more quickly slide down the descending limb of the inverted-U curve when demands are high. These frameworks collectively motivate the computational predictions outlined after we review the relevant physiological mechanisms. Together, these accounts make distinct, parameter-level predictions about how performance should change under effort. If high effort pushes older adults into supra-optimal arousal or resource competition, evidence quality should degrade (lower drift rate, \(v\)). If pupil-linked effort/arousal functions as a control/conflict signal, older adults may respond more cautiously (higher boundary separation, \(a\)). If concurrent grip maintenance interferes with encoding or motor output, peripheral processes may slow (higher non-decision time, \(t_0\)). Finally, if effort/phasic arousal reduces pre-existing response tendencies, starting-point bias may move toward neutrality (\(z \rightarrow 0.5\)). We test these predictions using hierarchical Bayesian diffusion modeling, and we use pupillometry to index the arousal state that is hypothesized to drive these shifts.

1.4 Arousal Dynamics and the Locus Coeruleus-Norepinephrine System

To ground these parameter-level predictions in physiology, we next focus on the Locus Coeruleus–Norepinephrine (LC–NE) system, a central regulator of arousal and neural gain (Aston-Jones & Cohen, 2005). Because LC–NE dynamics are observable via pupillometry, this framework provides a mechanistic bridge between effort-induced arousal and diffusion-model parameters. The LC-NE system modulates the “neural gain” of cortical circuits—essentially the signal-to-noise ratio of information processing. According to Adaptive Gain Theory (AGT), optimal performance relies on a balance between two modes of LC activity: a moderate tonic (baseline) firing rate that promotes focused attention, and robust phasic (event-related) bursts that facilitate rapid behavioral responses to task-relevant stimuli (Aston-Jones & Cohen, 2005; Gilzenrat et al., 2010).

In aging, LC structure and NE regulation change in ways that can compress the arousal–performance function described above, increasing susceptibility to supra-optimal arousal under demanding conditions (Huang & Clewett, 2024; Mather & Harley, 2016). The LC–NE system provides a mechanistic pathway through which effort-induced arousal can perturb decision computations.

Pupillometry provides a powerful, non-invasive window into these LC-NE dynamics. Because pupil diameter tracks LC firing activity with high temporal precision (Joshi et al., 2016; Murphy et al., 2014), it serves as a proxy for both tonic and phasic arousal states.

  • Baseline Pupil Diameter: Reflects tonic LC activity and general alertness levels (Gilzenrat et al., 2010).

  • Task-Evoked Pupil Response (TEPR): Reflects phasic LC activation and the mobilization of mental effort during task execution (Beatty, 1982). Specifically, the amplitude and latency of the TEPR—often quantified as the Area Under the Curve (AUC)—have been linked to the subjective difficulty of a task and the cognitive resources recruited to perform it (Kahneman & Beatty, 1966; Van Gerven et al., 2004).

Crucially, recent computational work has begun to bridge the gap between these physiological arousal signals and the latent cognitive processes of decision-making. In a seminal study, Cavanagh et al. (2014) demonstrated that eye tracking and pupillometry serve as indicators of dissociable latent decision processes. By applying hierarchical Bayesian DDM to a probabilistic learning task, they found that while gaze dwell time predicted the rate of evidence accumulation (drift rate), pupil dilation specifically predicted an increase in the decision threshold (\(a\)) during high-conflict choices. This finding fundamentally reframed the role of phasic arousal in decision-making: rather than merely “energizing” the system generally, the pupil-linked arousal response can act as a specific signal for cognitive control, prompting a “hold your horses” mechanism (Frank, 2006) that raises the decision boundary to prevent impulsive errors. This link between pupil dilation and threshold adjustment (\(a\)) has since been corroborated by others, suggesting it may be a general marker of decision uncertainty and conflict monitoring (Urai et al., 2017; Wel & Steenbergen, 2018).

This connection is vital for the present study. By integrating pupillometry with DDM, we can move beyond simple behavioral outcomes to ask mechanistic questions about how physical effort impacts the aging brain. Recent work has demonstrated that pupil dilation can serve as a trial-by-trial predictor of latent DDM parameters in young adults. Cavanagh and colleagues (2014) used hierarchical Bayesian DDM estimation to show a double dissociation: eye gaze dwell time predicted drift rate (evidence accumulation), whereas pupil dilation specifically predicted increases in decision threshold during high-conflict choices—particularly in “win-win” scenarios where multiple rewarding options required a “hold your horses” mechanism to prevent impulsive responding. This pupil-threshold coupling was interpreted as reflecting a neural conflict-detection system that signals the need for greater response caution.

Building on this framework, the present study extends the pupil-DDM approach in two critical ways: (1) we examine older adults, whose altered arousal regulation may produce different parameter-level effects, and (2) we manipulate arousal via physical effort rather than cognitive conflict, allowing us to test whether physical effort-induced arousal acts as a beneficial gain signal or a conflict alarm. Does the physical-induced arousal from a high-effort handgrip act as a beneficial boost that sharpens neural gain (increasing drift rate, \(v\)), as Adaptive Gain Theory might predict for moderate arousal? Or, does it trigger a conflict signal that prompts older adults to become more conservative (increasing threshold, \(a\)), as suggested by Cavanagh et al.’s (2014) findings in young adults during cognitive conflict? Alternatively, if the effort pushes older adults into a supra-optimal state, does the pupil signal reflect internal noise that degrades evidence quality (decreasing \(v\))?

This combined pupil-DDM approach allows us to directly test these competing accounts by linking observable physiological states to the latent computational components of the decision process. Critically, these frameworks generate separable predictions at the level of diffusion parameters. Resource competition accounts predict reduced evidence quality under high effort (\(v \downarrow\)). Conflict-control or “hold your horses” accounts predict increased response caution (\(a \uparrow\)) and, potentially, modest slowing outside the decision process due to concurrent motor demands (\(t_0 \uparrow\)). Finally, if phasic LC–NE responses reduce pre-existing response tendencies, starting-point bias may shift toward neutrality (\(z \rightarrow 0.5\)). This parameter-level separability is exactly what motivates the pupil-informed hierarchical Bayesian DDM approach used here and leads directly to the predictions formalized below.

1.4.1 Hypotheses and Predictions

Grounded in the behavioral and physiological frameworks above, we test four primary predictions.

  1. Drift rate (\(v\)) will decrease under high effort (40% MVC) relative to low effort, reflecting degraded evidence accumulation from resource competition or supra-optimal arousal.
  2. Boundary separation (\(a\)) will increase under high effort, reflecting the conflict-control signal suggested by phasic pupil-linked arousal and older adults’ strategic caution.
  3. Non-decision time (\(t_0\)) may increase modestly under high effort, reflecting cognitive-motor interference during concurrent grip maintenance. [Note: Model stability constraints precluded testing this hypothesis directly; see Results Section 3.6.]
  4. Starting bias (\(z\)) may shift toward 0.5 under high effort if strong phasic LC–NE responses reduce pre-existing response tendencies (i.e., a bias “reset”) (Gee et al., 2020).

1.5 Conclusion and Overview of the Present Study

In this chapter, we investigate how effort-induced arousal modulates decision-making in older adults at a computational level. By applying hierarchical Bayesian drift-diffusion modeling (HDDM) to behavioral data from older participants under low-effort vs. high-effort conditions, we test whether heightened arousal degrades evidence accumulation, triggers compensatory increases in boundary separation, alters bias, or slows non-decision processes. By decomposing older adults’ performance with the DDM, we can pinpoint the locus of the arousal effect: is it degrading the evidence itself, shifting the strategic criterion, resetting biases, or altering peripheral processing speeds? This approach allows us to move beyond simple outcome measures (mean reaction time or accuracy) to visualize the mechanism of how a physiologically loaded aging brain arrives at a choice. The broader significance of this work lies in understanding whether the characteristic cautiousness and processing inefficiencies of older decision-makers are fixed traits or dynamic features modulated by physiological state. If arousal can “tune” decision parameters in predictable ways, it suggests that decision performance in aging is not static but state-dependent. If excessive effort proves detrimental as hypothesized, it highlights the critical importance of effort regulation and stress management for older individuals in demanding environments. Ultimately, by mathematically decomposing these effects, we seek to clarify whether boosting arousal in older adults helps “overclock” their decision processes or instead exacerbates underlying capacity limits. This knowledge contributes to a more comprehensive theory of cognitive aging—one that accounts for both the baseline architectural changes of the brain and the dynamic, moment-to-moment influence of internal physiological states.

2 Methods

2.1 Participants and Experimental Design

2.1.1 Participants

Participants were recruited from communities surrounding the University of California, Riverside, through multiple channels including direct mail, posted flyers, social media advertisements, and word-of-mouth referrals. Eligibility criteria required participants to be aged 60–90 years, possess normal or corrected-to-normal vision and hearing, demonstrate sufficient English language comprehension to follow experimental instructions, and exhibit right-hand dominance. Exclusion criteria comprised contraindications for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), current use of psychotropic medications, clinically significant neurological disorders, and major systemic health conditions including diabetes mellitus.

Sixty-nine individuals initially enrolled in the study. Two participants were excluded following enrollment: one due to suspected mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and another due to possible essential tremor, yielding a final sample of 67 older adults (age: M = 71.3 years, SD = 4.8, range = 65+ years). Sample size was determined through a priori power analysis using G*Power 3.1 (Faul et al., 2007). For a repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) comparing two within-subject conditions (low vs. high grip force), assuming a medium effect size (f = 0.25), alpha level of 0.05, correlation of 0.5 between repeated measures, and desired statistical power of 0.80, the analysis indicated that a minimum of 34 participants was necessary. Twelve participants performed at or below chance level (\(\leq\) 55% accuracy) in some experimental conditions but were retained in analyses to maximize sample size, as hierarchical modeling approaches borrow statistical strength across participants to stabilize individual-level parameter estimates.

All participants provided written informed consent and received either course credit or monetary compensation for their participation. Study procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of California, Riverside, and all experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with approved guidelines and regulations.

2.1.2 Stimuli and Task Design

Task overview Participants completed two response-signal same/different discrimination tasks—an Auditory Detection Task (ADT) and a Visual Detection Task (VDT)—each performed under concurrent hand-grip effort manipulation. In both tasks, a standard and probe stimulus were presented in succession, and participants judged whether the stimuli were the same or different using button presses, followed by a confidence rating.

Trial structure. Both tasks shared the same response-signal timeline (see Figure 3). Each trial began with a jittered blank interval (1.5–4.5 s) displaying only a gray background. Participants were then shown a 3-second rectangular grip gauge with a marker positioned at either 5% or 40% from the bottom, indicating the target force level to be maintained using a hand-held dynamometer (left hand) at the corresponding percentage of their maximum voluntary contraction (MVC). MVC was determined during a brief calibration procedure prior to each task. Following the grip period, a 0.25-second blank screen and 0.5-second fixation dot preceded stimulus presentation.

Auditory Detection Task (ADT). The standard stimulus was a 0.1-second sinusoidal tone at 1000 Hz. After a 0.5-second inter-stimulus interval, the probe stimulus was presented as a 0.1-second tone that was either identical to the standard (\(\Delta frequency\) = 0) or positively offset by \(\Delta frequency \in \{8, 16, 32, 64\}\,\mathrm{Hz}\). For drift-diffusion model (DDM) analyses, the four non-zero offsets were collapsed into two difficulty bins: Hard = {8, 16} Hz and Easy = {32, 64} Hz, with Standard = 0 Hz.

Visual Detection Task (VDT). The standard stimulus was a centrally presented sinusoidal Gabor patch (spatial frequency 1.5 cycles/degree, Michelson contrast 0.20, diameter 4°, duration 0.1 s). After a 0.5-second inter-stimulus interval, the probe stimulus was presented as a 0.1-second Gabor that was either identical to the standard (\(\Delta contrast\) = 0) or showed a positive contrast increase of \(\Delta contrast \in \{0.06, 0.12, 0.24, 0.48\}\), with all other parameters held constant. For DDM analyses, offsets were collapsed into Hard = {0.06, 0.12} and Easy = {0.24, 0.48}, with Standard = 0.

Response collection. Following stimulus offset, participants relaxed their grip and had a 3-second response window to indicate whether the two stimuli were the same or different using their right hand (index finger = “different”; middle finger = “same”). This was immediately followed by another 3-second window in which participants provided a confidence rating on a 4-point scale (1 = low confidence, 4 = high confidence).

Trial counts and monitoring. Each task comprised 150 trials distributed across five blocks of 30 trials each. Within each block, the five stimulus levels (standard plus four offsets) were crossed with the two effort conditions (5% vs. 40% MVC) and presented in pseudorandom order with equal proportions (three repetitions per stimulus × effort combination). Hand-grip performance was monitored after each block, and participants received corrective feedback if they consistently failed to maintain the dynamometer at the designated force level (near the grip gauge marker) and/or for the correct duration (until offset of the probe stimulus).

2.1.3 Equipment

The experimental task was programmed and executed on an Alienware laptop. Visual stimuli were presented via a PROPixx projector (VPixx Technologies, Montreal, Canada; 480 Hz refresh rate, 1920×1080 resolution) and viewed through a head coil-mounted mirror (viewing distance \(\approx\) 12 cm). Auditory stimuli were delivered via MRI-safe earbuds (Newmatic Medical, Duluth, GA; 30 dB NRR). Behavioral responses were recorded using a RESPONSEpixx fiber-optic response box, hand-grip force via a MRI-compatible dynamometer (Current Designs Inc., Philadelphia, USA; 100 Hz sampling), and pupil diameter via an MRI-compatible TRACKPixx binocular eye tracker (VPixx Technologies, Montreal, Canada; 2000 Hz sampling). Stimuli were generated using Psychophysics Toolbox 3.0.18 (Brainard & Vision, 1997; Kleiner et al., 2007) in MATLAB R2022b (The MathWorks, Natick, MA). Data were collected inside an MRI scanner; neuroimaging results are reported elsewhere.

2.1.4 Data Processing and Quality Control

Reaction time definition. In this response-signal design, reaction time (RT) was initially recorded as the latency from response-screen onset (“Same or Different?” prompt) to button press, termed cue-locked RT. However, perceptual evidence becomes available at probe onset (the second stimulus). To align RT with evidence accumulation onset, we derived probe-onset-locked RT by adding the fixed post-probe delay to cue-locked RT:

\[RT_{\text{probe-onset}} = RT_{\text{cue-locked}} + 0.35\text{ s}. \tag{3}\]

where 0.35 s comprises 0.10 s probe duration plus 0.25 s grip-relaxation period. Probe-onset-locked RT served as the primary timing measure for DDM interpretation, as it aligns the decision process with evidence onset. Under this convention, the DDM non-decision time parameter (\(t_0\)) includes the fixed 0.35 s response-gating delay plus residual encoding and motor components. Cue-locked RT was retained for sensitivity analyses. This probe-onset-locked timing is our chosen convention to align \(t_0\) with evidence onset; some DDM studies alternatively use cue-locked or response-locked reference points depending on task structure.

Trial exclusion criteria. To minimize the influence of premature or accidental button presses, trials with cue-locked RT < 0.20 s were excluded from the primary analysis. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using alternative cutoffs of 0.15 s and 0.25 s. Additional exclusions included missed responses, invalid run performance, and trials with non-positive or missing RT values. The same exclusion criteria were applied to both cue-locked and probe-onset-locked RT datasets (which differ only by the fixed 0.35 s offset). For pupil-linked DDM models, trials additionally required valid pupil data (see Pupillometry Preprocessing section). The data processing pipeline is summarized in Table 2.

2.1.5 Experimental Design and Statistical Modeling

Design. The study employed a 2 (Task: ADT, VDT) × 3 (Difficulty: Standard, Easy, Hard) × 2 (Effort: Low, High) within-subjects factorial design, yielding 12 experimental conditions per participant. Because difficulty is determined by stimulus offset magnitude and thus unknown at trial onset, it was not included as a predictor of starting-point bias in the primary DDM specification.

Modeling approach. ADT and VDT were modeled jointly in a hierarchical framework with task as a fixed effect. This specification estimates task-specific parameter offsets while sharing subject-level random-effect variance across tasks, allowing hierarchical shrinkage to stabilize individual-level estimates.

2.2 Pupillometry Preprocessing and AUC Features

Recording and hardware. Pupil diameter was continuously recorded using an MRI-compatible TRACKPixx binocular eye tracker (VPixx Technologies, Montreal, Canada) sampling at 2000 Hz. Raw eye-tracking data were exported as .mat files and processed through a multi-stage preprocessing pipeline designed to ensure high-quality pupillometry measurements.

Artifact correction and quality control. Blink artifacts and invalid samples were removed using the ET-remove-artifacts MATLAB toolbox (Huang et al., 2020), which employs a velocity-based detection algorithm configured with a 100th-order low-pass filter (passband: 10 Hz, stopband: 12 Hz) and peak/trough threshold factors of 5 standard deviations above baseline. Artifacts exceeding 2 seconds in duration were replaced with missing values rather than interpolated, following best practices for handling extended periods of signal loss. All recordings then underwent manual inspection using the toolbox’s interactive plot editor to identify and correct any remaining undetected artifacts.

Trial alignment and downsampling. Cleaned pupil data were trial-aligned using event timestamps synchronized with behavioral event markers, then downsampled from 2000 Hz to 250 Hz using an 8th-order anti-aliasing filter to preserve signal quality while reducing computational demands. Baseline correction was applied by subtracting the mean pupil diameter during the baseline window (-0.5 s to 0 s relative to squeeze onset) from all timepoints in the trial.

AUC window (W3.0) and rationale. We computed trial-wise cognitive AUC from baseline-corrected pupil diameter in a window anchored to probe onset (the second stimulus; evidence onset). The primary window, W3.0, is a 3.0-s window spanning probe + 0.3 s to probe + 3.3 s (4.65 s to 7.65 s relative to squeeze onset), using trapezoidal integration. The +0.3 s offset accounts for pupil latency, and the 3.0 s duration captures the bulk of the task-evoked pupil response (TEPR), which peaks at ~2.95 s after probe onset. Because the response prompt occurs 0.35 s after probe onset, most of the TEPR unfolds during the response period; accordingly, W3.0 should be interpreted as TEPR magnitude during decision/response rather than a strictly pre-response predictor.

Figure 5 shows the response-signal timing structure used in analyses.

Confounds and sensitivity checks. We addressed potential post-response and motor confounds through multiple approaches. In trial-level pupil–DDM coupling models, RT was included as a covariate to control for decision state and prevent RT-driven pupil confounds. We also defined an optional motor-buffered variant of W3.0 that truncates the window 150 ms before the button press (\(t_{\mathrm{resp}} - 0.15\,\mathrm{s}\)); these buffered AUCs require re-processing the flat files with RT-aligned pupil traces and were computed for sensitivity analyses. Primary analyses use W3.0.

Pupil-linked DDM extensions (trial-level phasic arousal). To test whether momentary arousal modulates evidence accumulation or response bias, we extended the behavioral DDM by adding a trial-level phasic pupil predictor. The primary pupil metric was a cleaned, baseline-corrected, and participant-standardized phasic response summarized per trial and z-scored within participant. We evaluated a nested sequence of pupil-linked models: (i) behavioral baseline (no pupil predictor), (ii) pupil predicting starting-point bias, and (iii) pupil predicting both drift and bias. Competing pupil models were compared using Pareto-smoothed importance sampling leave-one-out cross-validation (PSIS‑LOO; an efficient approximation to standard LOO-CV), and inference focused on posterior distributions of pupil coefficients on the link scale as well as condition-level posterior predictions. Results from these pupil-linked models are reported in the Results section.

2.3 Drift Diffusion Modeling (DDM)

2.3.1 Decision coding and parameterization

Response-side coding rationale. We employed response-side coding (also called “stimulus coding” or “response coding”) where the upper boundary corresponds to “different” responses and the lower boundary to “same” responses (see Figure 1), rather than accuracy-based coding where boundaries represent correct/incorrect responses. This specification is necessary to disentangle response bias (a preference for one response alternative regardless of stimulus truth) from discriminability (drift rate) (Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008; Wiecki et al., 2013). In accuracy-based coding, bias would imply a preference for being correct (which is conceptually trivial), whereas response-side coding allows us to model the meaningful response preference for the “same” option commonly observed in detection tasks. This is particularly important for same/different discrimination where participants often exhibit response-specific biases (e.g., a conservative “same” bias) rather than general accuracy biases. Previous work has demonstrated that phasic arousal reduces such response biases in detection tasks (Gee et al., 2020), and capturing this effect requires mapping boundaries to response alternatives.

Implementation and model leverage. Response-side coding was implemented by creating a binary response indicator, coded as 1 for “different” responses and 0 for “same” responses, ensuring accurate mapping to DDM boundary assignments. Standard (Δ=0) trials provide critical leverage for identifying bias under this coding scheme, as they isolate response preference from evidence strength; descriptive choice proportions and model-implied patterns are reported in Results.

Link functions: To ensure parameter constraints, we apply link functions (Bürkner, 2017; Ratcliff & Tuerlinckx, 2002). The model was implemented using the brms package with family = wiener(link_bs="log", link_ndt="log", link_bias="logit"): drift rate \(v = \beta_v\) (identity link); boundary separation \(a = \exp(\beta_{\text{bs}})\) (log link, ensures \(a > 0\)); non-decision time \(t_0 = \exp(\beta_{\text{ndt}})\) (log link, ensures \(t_0 > 0\)); starting-point bias \(z = \text{logit}^{-1}(\beta_{\text{bias}}) = \frac{\exp(\beta_{\text{bias}})}{1 + \exp(\beta_{\text{bias}})}\) (logit link, ensures \(z \in [0,1]\)).

Hierarchical structure: For subject \(i\) and trial \(j\), the model parameters are specified as (Bürkner, 2017; Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008):

\[v_{ij} = \beta_{v,0} + \sum_k \beta_{v,k} X_{k,ij} + u_{v,i} \tag{4}\]

\[a_{ij} = \exp\left(\beta_{\text{bs},0} + \sum_k \beta_{\text{bs},k} X_{k,ij} + u_{\text{bs},i}\right) \tag{5}\]

\[t_{0,ij} = \exp\left(\beta_{\text{ndt},0} + \sum_k \beta_{\text{ndt},k} X_{k,ij}\right) \tag{6}\]

\[z_{ij} = \text{logit}^{-1}\left(\beta_{\text{bias},0} + \sum_k \beta_{\text{bias},k} X_{k,ij} + u_{\text{bias},i}\right) \tag{7}\]

where \(\beta_{0}\) are population-level intercepts, \(\beta_k\) are population-level coefficients for predictors \(X_k\) (e.g., task, effort condition, difficulty level), and \(u_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2_u)\) are subject-level random effects. Note that \(t_0\) is modeled without subject-level random effects to maintain model stability in the response-signal design.

Likelihood: The likelihood for a single trial with RT \(t\) and decision \(d \in \{\text{"same"}, \text{"different"}\}\) follows the Wiener first-passage time distribution (Feller, 1968):

\[p(t, d | v, a, t_0, z) = \text{Wiener}(t - t_0 | v, a, z) \tag{8}\]

where the Wiener distribution gives the probability density of the first-passage time to boundary \(d\) given drift \(v\), boundary separation \(a\), and starting point \(z \cdot a\).

Computational implementation. All analyses were performed in R (R Core Team, 2025) using brms (Bürkner, 2017, 2018) with CmdStan (Stan Development Team, 2024) via cmdstanr (Gabry & Češnovar, 2021). Model fitting was conducted on a cloud compute instance (Google Cloud Platform) to accommodate the computational demands of hierarchical Wiener models. Model comparison used leave-one-out cross-validation via loo (Vehtari et al., 2017). Data processing used the tidyverse suite (Wickham, 2016; Wickham, François, et al., 2023; Wickham, Vaughan, et al., 2023; Wickham et al., 2024), tables were generated with gt (Iannone et al., 2024), and posterior analysis used posterior (Bürkner et al., 2022). The manuscript was rendered using Quarto (Allaire et al., 2022).

2.3.2 Model specification (standard-only calibration and primary model)

Standard-only bias calibration model. To isolate bias identification from drift, we fit a hierarchical Wiener DDM to Standard trials only (~3.6k trials under the primary filter). Starting-point bias (z) was allowed to vary by Task and Effort (known pre-trial) but constrained to be constant across Difficulty levels, as trial difficulty was randomized and unknown at decision onset. This specification reflects the causal structure: participants cannot adjust their starting point based on an unknown future event.

Parameter-specific formulas were: drift (v): rt | dec(dec_upper) ~ 1 + (1|subject_id) with relaxed prior N(0, 2) to allow potential negative drift; boundary (bs): bs ~ 1 + (1|subject_id); non-decision time (ndt): ndt ~ 1 (intercept-only); bias (z): bias ~ task + effort_condition + (1|subject_id). These formulas comprise one model fitted simultaneously using brms’s bf() function.

Primary analysis model (behavioral DDM; no pupil). The primary behavioral DDM was fit to probe-onset-locked RTs (derived by adding 0.35 s to cue-locked RT) using the 0.20 s cue-locked lower-bound filter. The model parameterization matched the experimental design: difficulty (standard/easy/hard), effort condition, and task (auditory vs visual). Drift rate (v) captured the quality and direction of evidence accumulation under response-side coding; boundary separation (bs) captured response caution; starting-point bias (bias) captured pre-decisional response preference; and non-decision time (ndt) captured residual encoding/motor components not attributable to accumulation on the probe-onset time scale.

  • Drift rate (v): modeled as a function of difficulty and effort (additive effects, no interaction).
  • Boundary separation (bs): modeled as a function of difficulty and effort (additive effects).
  • Starting-point bias (bias): modeled as a function of task and effort (additive effects, no interaction), to quantify systematic response-side preferences that may differ across modality and effort state.
  • Non-decision time (ndt): modeled conservatively as intercept-only on the probe-onset time scale. Non-decision time (\(t_0\)) was constrained to be intercept-only based on pilot analyses indicating poor identification of condition-specific estimates in the response-signal paradigm (see Results Section 3.6 for justification). The probe-onset-locked definition already incorporates a fixed post-stimulus buffer and the response-signal structure complicates interpreting additional ndt modulation. Random intercepts by participant were included for v, bs, and bias; ndt was modeled as fixed-only to improve identifiability in the response-signal setting and to reduce parameter trade-offs.

Note: Task was not included as a predictor for drift rate or boundary separation in the primary model specification for two reasons: (1) the primary research question concerned effort-induced arousal modulation of decision parameters, and (2) we aimed to reduce model complexity while retaining adequate power to detect effort effects. To verify that this simplification did not obscure meaningful task differences or interactions, we conducted a sensitivity analysis including Task as a predictor (Appendix~A5.3). Parameter estimates are therefore reported pooled across tasks in the main text, with task-specific estimates available in Appendix~A5.3.

RT timing convention. We compared two RT definitions: (1) cue-locked RT, measured from response-window onset (when the response signal appears), and (2) probe-onset-locked RT, measured from probe stimulus onset, incorporating the fixed 0.35-s post-probe delay before the response window opens. We selected probe-onset-locked RT as our primary timing convention for two reasons. First, this definition aligns non-decision time (\(t_0\)) with the onset of evidence accumulation at the probe stimulus, providing a more interpretable parameterization of the DDM. Second, the probe-onset timing captures the complete decision-relevant interval from stimulus presentation through response execution. The expected rightward shift of approximately 0.35 s in the probe-onset RT distribution relative to cue-locked RT (Figure 9) serves as a validation check that the timing transformation was implemented correctly. All primary DDM analyses use probe-onset-locked RT; cue-locked RT served as a robustness check in sensitivity analyses (Appendix~A5.2).

Sensitivity fits. To verify robustness, we refit the behavioral DDM under (i) cue-locked RT timing and (ii) alternative cue-locked RT lower bounds (0.15 s and 0.25 s). These sensitivity results are summarized briefly in the main Results and documented in full in the Appendix.

Primary DDM results. Primary DDM results are reported for probe-onset-locked RT with 0.20 s cutoff; cue-locked and 0.15/0.25 s threshold variants are sensitivity fits reported in the Appendix.

Figure 6 provides a visual summary of how experimental predictors map onto each DDM parameter in the primary model, highlighting the key constraint that starting-point bias (z) does not vary by difficulty because trial difficulty is randomized and unknown before evidence accumulation begins.

2.3.3 Manipulation Checks

To confirm the experimental manipulations worked as intended, we conducted mixed-effects analyses on accuracy and RT independent of any DDM assumptions. These analyses serve to validate that the experimental design successfully manipulated difficulty and effort conditions before proceeding to DDM parameter estimation.

Accuracy analysis. Accuracy was modeled using mixed-effects logistic regression (generalized linear mixed model, GLMM) with fixed effects for difficulty, effort, and task (including interactions as needed), and random intercepts for subject. Analyses were restricted to Easy and Hard trials only (excluding Standard trials), as the difficulty manipulation is only meaningful within “different” trials where stimulus offsets vary.

Reaction time analysis. Reaction time was modeled using mixed-effects linear regression on cue-locked RT (median RT per subject-condition), with the same fixed and random effects structure as the accuracy model. Standard trials were excluded to focus on trials where difficulty manipulation is meaningful.

These manipulation checks confirm that the experimental manipulations affect performance as intended and justify the modeling choices for the DDM analysis. Results from these analyses are reported in the Results section (see “Manipulation Checks: Accuracy and RT”).

2.3.4 Priors, Computation, and Diagnostics

Priors. All priors were weakly informative and specified on the link scale. For intercepts, drift rate (\(v\)) used Normal(0, 1); boundary separation (\(a\)) used Normal(log(1.7), 0.30), corresponding to \(a \approx 1.7\) on the natural scale; non-decision time (\(t_0\)) used Normal(log(0.50), 0.15), corresponding to \(t_0 \approx 0.50\) s on the natural scale (probe-onset-locked timing); and starting-point bias (\(z\)) used Normal(0, 0.5), corresponding to \(z \approx 0.5\) (no bias) on the probability scale. For slope parameters, drift rate slopes used Normal(0, 0.6–0.7), boundary separation slopes used Normal(0, 0.25–0.30), and bias slopes used Normal(0, 0.35). Random effects were assigned Student-t(3, 0, 0.30) priors for standard deviations and LKJ(2) priors for correlations. Prior versus posterior distributions for \(t_0\) are shown in Supplementary Figure 20, confirming adequate identifiability for the group-level intercept despite the response-signal design.

MCMC sampling. Models were estimated using the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) (Hoffman & Gelman, 2014) with 8 parallel chains, each run for 2,000 iterations (1,000 warmup, 1,000 sampling). Within-chain parallelism was enabled (4 threads per chain) to accelerate sampling on a multi-core compute instance. Sampling controls were set to adapt_delta = 0.95 and max_treedepth = 12 to balance convergence and efficiency. Convergence was assessed using \(\hat{R}\) \(\leq\) 1.01 (Gelman & Rubin, 1992; Vehtari et al., 2021b) and minimum bulk effective sample size (ESS) \(\geq\) 400. Trace plots were visually inspected for all parameters, and divergent transitions were monitored. Pre-specified convergence criteria required maximum \(\hat{R}\) \(\leq\) 1.01, minimum bulk and tail ESS \(\geq\) 400, and zero divergent transitions. All reported models satisfied these criteria (maximum \(\hat{R} < 1.01\), minimum ESS \(> 1200\), zero divergences).

Model comparison. We compared a small set of pre-specified behavioral DDM variants differing in (i) parameterization of experimental effects (baseline vs. additive) and (ii) RT timing convention (cue-locked vs. probe-onset-locked). Models were compared using Pareto-smoothed importance sampling leave-one-out cross-validation (PSIS-LOO) via the loo package (Vehtari et al., 2017). We report expected log predictive density (ELPD) and its standard error, with model differences assessed using loo_compare(), which computes the standard error of the ELPD difference accounting for correlations between models fitted to the same data (Vehtari et al., 2017). Differences in ELPD exceeding 4 are considered meaningful (approximately equivalent to Δ LOOIC > 8). Pareto-k diagnostics were inspected to assess reliability of PSIS-LOO approximations. The LOO comparison table is presented in Results (Table 7), with the full comparison plot in Appendix~A1 (Figure 21).

PSIS diagnostics. We assessed the reliability of LOO estimates using Pareto-k diagnostics. Following Vehtari et al. (2017), we flagged observations with \(k > 0.7\) as potentially problematic for PSIS approximation. When aggregate Pareto-\(k_{\max}\) exceeded 0.7, we report model comparisons with appropriate caution and prioritize parameter-level inference over model selection.

Posterior predictive checks. Model fit was evaluated using posterior predictive checks (PPC), including subject-level and unconditional comparisons between observed and predicted data. PPC diagnostics included quantile-probability (QP) plots, Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) statistics, and residual heatmaps. Pre-declared PPC thresholds were: subject-wise mid-body QP RMSE \(\leq\) 0.09 s, |Δ accuracy| \(\leq\) 0.05, KS statistic \(\leq\) 0.15, and \(\leq\) 15% of cells flagged. Detailed posterior predictive checks and visual diagnostics are provided in Appendix~A3 (see Section 5.4.4–Section 5.4.6 and Figure 32).

3 Results

3.1 Data inclusion and preprocessing summary

The DDM analysis included 67 older adults (\(N_{ADT}\) = 64, \(N_{VDT}\) = 65 after task-specific exclusions). We compared four model specifications crossing two RT definitions (cue-locked vs. probe-onset-locked) and two model structures (baseline vs. additive). The primary additive model with probe-onset-locked RT retained 18,097 trials after applying the 0.20 s RT threshold (Table 3). All models showed excellent convergence (max \(\hat{R}\) < 1.01, all divergent transitions = 0, minimum bulk ESS > 1200), with the additive probe-onset-locked model selected based on LOO cross-validation (Section 3.5).

3.2 Manipulation Checks: Accuracy and RT

We confirmed that the difficulty and effort manipulations affected performance as intended using mixed-effects models independent of DDM assumptions, restricted to Easy and Hard trials (N = 13,771).

Hard trials were significantly less accurate than Easy trials, \(\beta = -2.97\), 95% CI [−3.07, −2.88], \(p < .001\), with mean accuracies of 30.5% (Hard) and 85.2% (Easy) (Table 4, Figure 7). Accuracy was lowest in Hard+High effort cells (26.4%), indicating that difficulty and effort effects combine additively. High-effort trials showed lower accuracy than low-effort trials (\(\beta = -0.15\), 95% CI [−0.23, −0.06], \(p = .001\)), with mean accuracy of 56.8% (High) versus 58.5% (Low).

Hard trials were slower than Easy trials, \(\beta = 0.23 \,\text{s}\), 95% CI [0.20, 0.27], \(p < .001\), with median RTs of 1.01 s and 0.75 s, respectively (Table 5, Figure 8). The effort manipulation did not reliably affect RT (\(\beta = 0.02 \,\text{s}\), 95% CI [−0.02, 0.05]).

Difficulty reliably affected both accuracy (\(\beta = -2.97\), 95% CI [−3.07, −2.88], \(p < .001\)) and RT (\(\beta = 0.23\) s, 95% CI [0.20, 0.27], \(p < .001\)). Effort reduced accuracy (\(\beta = -0.15\), 95% CI [−0.23, −0.06], \(p = .001\)) without reliably affecting RT (\(\beta = 0.02\) s, 95% CI [−0.02, 0.05]). These patterns were consistent with successful experimental manipulation of perceptual difficulty and concurrent physical effort.

3.2.1 Behavioral Performance

Across Easy and Hard trials, older adults showed moderate overall accuracy (mean = 63.5%, SD = 10.5%; Table 6). When including Standard trials (which had near-perfect performance), overall accuracy was 70.1%. Accuracy varied substantially across individuals (range = 30.6–92.3%), consistent with the strong effects of difficulty and effort documented above.

3.2.2 RT timing validation

As described in Methods, we adopted probe-onset-locked RT as our primary timing convention. Figure 9 confirms the expected 0.35-s rightward shift between cue-locked and probe-onset-locked RT distributions, validating correct implementation of the timing transformation. The probe-onset distribution incorporates the fixed post-probe delay (0.35 s) plus the response-window RT component, whereas cue-locked RT reflects only the response-window component. Subsequent DDM analyses use probe-onset-locked RT, with cue-locked RT serving as a sensitivity check (Appendix~A5.2).

3.3 Convergence and model fit diagnostics

For the primary additive model with probe-onset-locked RT, all parameters satisfied \(\hat{R} \leq 1.01\), bulk ESS \(\geq 400\), and there were no divergent transitions. Posterior predictive checks indicated adequate fit to the central portions of the RT distributions, with some misfit in fast tails (particularly for Easy/VDT conditions; Appendix~A3).

3.4 Model comparison: Baseline vs. additive specification

We compared a baseline model (intercept-only for all parameters) against an additive model (difficulty and effort as predictors) using Leave-One-Out cross-validation (LOO-CV). Both models were fit to the same dataset (probe-onset-locked RT, ≥0.20 s threshold, N = 18,097 trials).

We selected probe-onset-locked RT as our primary timing convention based on theoretical interpretability: this definition aligns non-decision time (\(t_0\)) with evidence onset at the probe stimulus. We did not use LOO comparison to select between cue-locked vs. probe-onset definitions, as these represent different outcome scales (differing by a fixed 0.35 s constant) and are not directly comparable via predictive metrics. Sensitivity analyses with cue-locked RT yielded substantively identical parameter estimates (Appendix~A5.2).

The additive model strongly outperformed the baseline model (Δ ELPD = 5,151, SE(Δ) = 169; Δ LOOIC = 10,302). This difference is approximately 30 standard errors from zero, providing overwhelming evidence that difficulty and effort meaningfully predict decision parameters. All subsequent analyses use the additive specification.

3.5 Behavioral DDM parameter estimates (no pupil)

The primary behavioral model (additive specification, probe-onset-locked RT, 0.20 s threshold) revealed that high effort reduced drift rate (\(v\)) relative to low effort, with minimal effects on boundary separation (\(a\)) or starting-point bias (\(z\)) (Table 8). Non-decision time (\(t_0\)) was modeled as constant across conditions (0.347 s; see below). Drift rates were strongly modulated by difficulty: Easy trials showed higher drift toward “different” responses than Hard trials, while Standard trials showed negative drift toward “same” responses (Table 8, Figure 10). Boundary separation (\(a\)) was larger for Hard than Easy trials (Table 8, Figure 11). Starting-point bias (\(z\)) exceeded 0.5 (neutral) in all conditions, indicating a systematic preference for “different” responses. This bias was modestly higher in VDT than ADT (Table 9, Figure 12).

Note: Coding convention: Positive \(v\) indicates drift toward the upper (“different”) boundary; negative \(v\) indicates drift toward the lower (“same”) boundary. Boundary separation (\(a\)) is reported on the natural scale (evidence units). Non-decision time (\(t_0\)) was modeled as intercept-only (constant across all conditions at 0.347 s) for model stability in the response-signal design (see text). Empirical pattern: Negative drift estimates on Standard and Hard trials reflect systematic bias toward “same” responses, consistent with participants treating small/absent stimulus offsets as evidence of similarity.

Non-decision time (\(t_0\)) modeling constraint. To ensure model stability, we constrained \(t_0\) to be constant across all conditions (\(t_0 = 0.347\) s, 95% CrI [0.346, 0.347]). Preliminary models allowing condition-specific \(t_0\) estimates (e.g., ndt ~ 1 + effort_condition) showed poor parameter identification, likely due to: (a) brief stimulus durations (100 ms) leaving minimal variance in encoding time, (b) post-stimulus grip release removing motor interference during the response window, and (c) the fixed probe-to-prompt delay (0.35 s) being constant across conditions. Model comparison via LOO favored the intercept-only specification (\(\Delta\)LOO = 12.3, SE = 5.2 against effort-varying \(t_0\)). While this precludes direct testing of Hypothesis 3 (cognitive-motor interference effects on peripheral processes), the accuracy decline under high effort (Table 4) was fully accounted for by changes in drift rate (\(v\)), without compensatory increases in boundary separation (\(a\)). Notably, RT did not reliably slow under high effort in manipulation checks (Table 5, \(\beta = 0.02\) s, 95% CI [−0.02, 0.05]), consistent with the absence of boundary adjustment in the DDM decomposition. Thus, any dual-task interference manifested in evidence accumulation rather than encoding or motor execution stages.

Together, these patterns show that task difficulty strongly modulates evidence accumulation (\(v\)) and caution (\(a\)), while bias (\(z\)) varies by task with minimal effort effects. Forest plots with fixed effects by task are in Appendix~A2 (Figure 25, Figure 26).

3.5.1 Task modality effects

Sensitivity analyses confirmed that the Effort → Drift effect was robust to Task inclusion (Appendix~A5.3). Although auditory and visual tasks differed in absolute drift rates (Task main effect \(\beta \approx 0.07\), 95% CrI [0.02, 0.12]), the Effort × Task interaction was not significant (\(\beta \approx 0.03\), 95% CrI [−0.03, 0.09], includes zero), indicating that high effort reduced drift rate similarly in both modalities.

Figure 13 shows drift rate estimates separately for ADT and VDT across all difficulty and effort conditions. The difficulty gradient (Standard \(<\) Hard \(<\) Easy) is consistent across both modalities, with VDT showing slightly lower absolute drift rates than ADT. Critically, the effort effect (reduction in drift under high effort) manifests similarly in both tasks, confirming that the cognitive-motor interference effect generalizes across sensory modalities.

Similarly, Figure 14 shows boundary separation across conditions and tasks. Difficulty strongly modulates caution (Easy \(<\) Standard \(<\) Hard) in both tasks, with minimal effort effects. The lack of an effort effect on boundary separation (discussed in Section 3.5.2) is consistent across both ADT and VDT, suggesting that older adults do not strategically adjust response caution under increased physical effort, regardless of sensory modality.

3.5.2 Key Contrasts

To evaluate our a priori predictions about how effort and difficulty modulate decision parameters, we examined posterior contrasts on the link scale (Appendix~A2, Table 19).

Contrasts on the link scale confirmed large effects of difficulty on drift rate (\(v\)), with Easy \(>\) Hard \(>\) Standard, and consistent effects of effort on \(v\). Boundary separation (\(a\)) and starting-point bias (\(z\)) showed more modest modulation by difficulty and effort (Appendix~A2, Table 19, Figure 25, Figure 26). Relative to the hypotheses in Section 1.4.1: Consistent with H1, high effort produced lower drift rates (\(v\)) than low effort. Contrary to H2, boundary separation (\(a\)) did not reliably increase under high effort. Regarding H3 (non-decision time under high effort): We could not test H3 directly, as \(t_0\) was modeled as constant across conditions for stability (see above). Exploratory models allowing \(t_0\) to vary by effort showed negligible and highly uncertain effects (\(\beta_{\text{effort}} = 0.02\), 95% CrI [−0.05, 0.09] on log scale, corresponding to \(\Delta t_0 < 10\) ms), suggesting peripheral processes were minimally affected by the grip manipulation. Contrary to H4, starting-point bias (\(z\)) showed minimal effort effect (Section 3.7).

3.6 Standard-only bias calibration on Standard trials

In a separate calibration model fit to Standard trials only, starting-point bias (\(z\)) was estimated independently of drift rate (\(v\)). Bias values were consistently above 0.5, indicating a tendency toward “different” responses. The task contrast (VDT−ADT) was \(\Delta z = -0.04\), 95% CrI [−0.06, −0.02], with VDT showing lower bias toward “different” responses than ADT, and minimal effort effect (Low−High \(\Delta z = -0.01\), 95% CrI [−0.02, 0.01]; Table 13, Figure 15).

To summarize how difficulty and effort jointly influenced all DDM parameters, Figure 16 shows integrated condition effects on \(a\), \(z\), and \(t_0\). Difficulty effects were strongest for boundary separation (\(a\)), with Easy trials showing reduced caution relative to Hard trials. Bias (\(z\)) varied by task and effort but not by difficulty, consistent with the design constraints described in the Methods.

The hierarchical structure revealed substantial individual differences in all DDM parameters, with drift rate showing the widest variability. 4 Discussion

4.1 Summary of Key Findings

Results confirmed Hypothesis 1 (H1): high effort reduced drift rate, consistent with resource competition or supra-optimal arousal (Section 4.2). Contrary to Hypothesis 2 (H2), boundary separation did not increase under high effort, contradicting conflict-control predictions (Section 4.4). Hypothesis 3 (H3) could not be tested directly due to model constraints requiring constant \(t_0\), though exploratory analyses suggested minimal peripheral slowing (Section 4.3). Importantly, while high effort reduced accuracy (manipulation check: \(\beta = -0.15\), \(p < .001\)), it did not reliably slow reaction time (\(\beta = 0.02\) s, 95% CI [−0.02, 0.05]). This dissociation—lower accuracy without RT slowing—is explained by the DDM decomposition: drift rate declined (evidence quality), but boundary separation did not increase (no compensatory caution), resulting in faster errors rather than slower correct responses. The Effort → Drift effect was robust to Task (ADT vs. VDT) inclusion (Appendix~A5.3). Trial-wise pupil-linked arousal (TEPR) did not reliably modulate drift rate or starting-point bias when included as predictors (Section 3.8). This pattern of results confirms the “detrimental impact” hypothesis grounded in Resource Competition theory (Azer et al., 2023; Wickens, 2008), which predicts that concurrent physical effort consumes shared cognitive resources, degrading the quality of evidence accumulation. However, the null effect on boundary separation challenges the “adaptive caution” hypothesis (Strategic Adaptation), which predicted that older adults would respond to increased internal noise by raising their decision thresholds to preserve accuracy.

4.2 The “Crunch Point”: Why Drift Rate Declined

Our finding that high physical effort significantly reduced drift rates—indicating slower and noisier information accumulation—challenges simple arousal-facilitation accounts and aligns more closely with resource-depletion models. Specifically, the observed decline in processing efficiency supports the Compensation-Related Utilization of Neural Circuits Hypothesis (CRUNCH) (Reuter-Lorenz & Cappell, 2008). The CRUNCH model posits that while older adults can effectively recruit compensatory neural resources to meet lower task demands, they hit a resource ceiling or “crunch point” at lower levels of objective difficulty than younger adults. Once this threshold is crossed, compensatory mechanisms fail, and performance declines precipitously. In the present study, the 40% MVC condition likely pushed participants beyond this critical tipping point. Rather than acting as a beneficial arousal boost that “sharpens” neural gain (as predicted by Adaptive Gain Theory for moderate levels; (Aston-Jones & Cohen, 2005)), the sustained high-effort requirement consumed the limited cognitive resources available for evidence accumulation, resulting in the observed degradation of drift rate.

4.3 Modeling Constraints: Non-Decision Time and Peripheral Processes

We initially hypothesized (H3) that concurrent grip maintenance would slow peripheral processes (perceptual encoding and motor execution), increasing non-decision time (\(t_0\)). However, preliminary model comparisons revealed that condition-specific \(t_0\) estimates were poorly identified in our response-signal design, necessitating an intercept-only specification (\(t_0 = 0.347\) s constant across conditions).

Several design factors contributed to this constraint:

  1. Brief stimulus durations (100 ms) left minimal variance in encoding time across conditions, making perceptual delays difficult to estimate separately from decision-related slowing.

  2. Post-stimulus grip release: Participants relaxed their grip immediately after probe offset and before the response window opened, removing direct motor interference during response execution. The 3-second response window began after grip release, meaning motor processes during the actual button press were not confounded with grip force.

  3. Response-signal timing: The probe-onset-locked RT definition includes a fixed 0.35-s delay (0.1 s probe + 0.25 s grip-relaxation period) before the response window, but this delay is constant across conditions. Any effort-related encoding differences would need to manifest within the 100-ms stimulus window, which may be too brief to detect reliably.

  4. Model identification: Exploratory models with ndt ~ 1 + effort_condition showed weak convergence (R̂ > 1.05 for effort slopes) and LOO comparison disfavored this specification (\(\Delta\)LOO = 12.3, SE = 5.2), indicating the data were insufficient to distinguish \(t_0\) variation from trial-level noise.

Importantly, the accuracy decline under high effort observed in behavioral analyses (Table 4) was fully captured by reductions in drift rate (\(v\)), not by changes in \(t_0\). RT did not reliably slow under high effort in manipulation checks (Table 5, \(\beta = 0.02\) s, 95% CI [−0.02, 0.05]). This pattern suggests that cognitive-motor interference manifested during evidence accumulation—the core decision process—rather than during peripheral encoding or motor stages. This interpretation aligns with resource competition theories (Wickens, 2008), which posit that concurrent physical and cognitive demands compete for shared central processing resources, degrading the quality of evidence extraction rather than slowing sensory or motor transmission.

While we cannot rule out small \(t_0\) effects below our detection threshold (< 10 ms), the dominance of drift rate changes indicates that the primary locus of effort-induced slowing was computational (evidence accumulation), not peripheral. Future studies using longer stimulus durations or continuous response paradigms (without post-stimulus grip release) may better isolate peripheral timing effects.

4.4 Strategic Rigidity: Why Caution (\(a\)) Didn’t Increase

Despite the internal noise introduced by high effort (as evidenced by reduced drift rates), older adults failed to dynamically adjust their decision criteria by increasing boundary separation (\(a\)). This null finding suggests strategic rigidity in aging: older adults may have difficulty flexibly modulating their decision thresholds in response to changing task demands, even when such adaptation would be beneficial. This rigidity could reflect reduced executive flexibility or a tendency to maintain a fixed “safety-first” strategy regardless of context. While older adults are generally risk-averse and prioritize accuracy (Starns & Ratcliff, 2010), the failure to increase caution under conditions of degraded evidence quality may indicate that the cognitive resources needed for strategic adjustment are themselves depleted by the dual-task demands of the high-effort condition.

4.5 Bias and Phasic Arousal

Regarding starting-point bias (\(z\)), our results revealed a consistent conservative bias across conditions, with a posterior mean of \(z = 0.63\) (95% CrI [0.58, 0.68]) on Standard trials, indicating a slight preference for “different” responses (Table 20). This bias was robust across effort conditions, with no significant effect of effort level (High vs. Low contrast: \(\Delta = -0.01\), 95% CrI [−0.02, 0.01], \(P(\Delta>0) = 0.27\)). However, we did observe a significant task difference: VDT showed less bias toward “different” than ADT (\(\Delta = -0.04\), 95% CrI [−0.06, −0.02], \(P(\Delta>0) < .001\); Table 13), suggesting modality-specific differences in response tendencies.

These findings can be interpreted in the context of LC-NE system dynamics. Recent work suggests that phasic arousal, indexed by pupil dilation, can suppress pre-existing choice biases, “resetting” the decision process to a neutral state (Gee et al., 2020). The lack of a significant effort effect on bias suggests that the high-effort manipulation (40% MVC) may not have elicited strong enough phasic arousal responses to modulate starting-point bias, or that any such effects were offset by other factors. Alternatively, the integrity of the LC-NE system in our older adult sample may have moderated the expected bias suppression (Huang & Clewett, 2024). The task-specific bias differences (VDT < ADT) may reflect inherent differences in how auditory versus visual detection tasks engage response strategies, independent of arousal state. Direct pupillometry integration (Section 3.7) did not reveal reliable modulation of bias or drift rate by trial-wise TEPR, consistent with the null pupil-DDM associations reported in Section 3.8.

4.6 Limitations and Future Directions

Our findings must be interpreted within the constraints of the response-signal design, where RTs are measured from response-screen onset rather than stimulus onset. This design constrains the interpretation of \(t_0\) to primarily reflect motor execution and response selection, excluding early perceptual/encoding processes. Despite these constraints, our results provide clear evidence that effort regulation is critical for older adults because they have a lower “tipping point” where effort becomes interference. The CRUNCH model and CMI framework together explain why the 40% MVC condition pushed older adults past their compensatory capacity, resulting in degraded processing efficiency (reduced drift), without the adaptive increase in caution that might have mitigated these effects. (Non-decision time \(t_0\) could not be tested for condition effects due to model stability constraints; see Section 4.3.) These findings underscore the importance of managing effort levels in real-world contexts where older adults must balance physical and cognitive demands.

4.6.1 Model Family Limitations

  1. Constant-drift Wiener DDM: The base Wiener DDM assumes constant drift within each trial and no across-trial variability in drift (sv), starting point (sz), or non-decision time (s\(t_0\)). This can underfit fast tails, especially in VDT-Hard conditions. The constant-drift Wiener DDM underfits fast RT tails, especially in VDT-Hard. Response-signal timing limits identifiability of across-trial variability. Future work could add a small contaminant mixture, across-trial variability (sv, sz), or urgency/collapsing bounds; LBA/race models may better capture fast-tail dynamics in the Easy/VDT regime.

  2. Non-decision time (\(t_0\)) intercept-only: In the response-signal design, \(t_0\) primarily reflects motor execution. We constrained \(t_0\) to be constant across conditions (intercept-only) due to poor identification of condition-specific estimates in pilot models (see Results Section 3.6 and Discussion Section 4.3). This precluded testing Hypothesis 3 (effort effects on peripheral processes).

  3. Alternative model families: Linear Ballistic Accumulator (LBA) or race models may provide better fit for fast-tail dynamics, particularly for Easy/VDT. These models allow for more flexible RT distributions and may better accommodate the response-signal design.

4.6.2 Design-Specific Limitations

  1. Response-signal RT measurement: RTs are measured from response-screen onset, not stimulus onset. This constrains the interpretation of \(t_0\) to motor execution and response selection, excluding early perceptual/encoding processes. While this is appropriate for the current design, it limits generalizability to traditional RT paradigms.

  2. Effort manipulation: Physical effort (grip force) may interact with motor execution in complex ways not fully captured by small fixed effects on \(t_0\). Future work integrating EMG or kinematic measures could provide richer insights into effort-motor interactions.

4.6.3 Misfit in Easy/VDT

  1. Fast-tail misfit: The most pronounced misfit occurs in Easy/VDT conditions, where the model underpredicts the frequency of very fast correct responses. This suggests a subset of trials may reflect:

    • Anticipatory responses (partially captured by 2% censoring)
    • A “fast-guess” process not represented in the base DDM
    • Extremely high drift rates that are incompatible with the assumed Wiener process for a small subset of trials

    Sensitivity analyses (2% censoring, unconditional PPCs) confirm that substantive conclusions are robust, but future work should explore mixture models or urgency signals to better account for these fast responses.

4.6.4 LOO Reliability for Pupil Models

  1. PSIS-LOO reliability for pupil-augmented models: Pareto-k diagnostics indicated that PSIS-LOO was unreliable for pupil-augmented models (\(k_{\max}\) = 0.91), likely due to influential observations where pupil responses were highly variable. This prevented definitive model comparison via LOO; future work should employ k-fold cross-validation or moment matching to obtain more stable estimates. Importantly, our conclusions about pupil effects are based on posterior parameter estimates (where TEPR coefficients were near-zero with wide credible intervals) rather than on LOO-based model selection.

  2. TEPR window sensitivity: We did not test sensitivity to TEPR window definition (e.g., W1.3 early window excluding motor-confounded periods) in the pupil-DDM coupling models; primary analyses used W3.0 only.

4.7 Conclusions

This chapter presents a comprehensive hierarchical Wiener DDM analysis of a response-signal change-detection task in older adults. The primary model, in which task difficulty modulates drift rate and boundary separation, with starting-point bias constant across difficulty levels (reflecting the randomized trial design), is strongly supported by LOO cross-validation and shows acceptable fit to subject-wise mid-body RT quantiles (see Appendix~A5 (Section 5.4.4–Section 5.4.6) for full diagnostics, including Figure 32). Key findings—difficulty effects on \(v\) and \(a\); task-specific differences in bias (\(z\)); and small effort effects—are robust across multiple sensitivity analyses. While the base Wiener DDM shows localized misfit in fast tails (especially Easy/VDT), this does not undermine the core substantive conclusions. Future extensions incorporating across-trial variability, urgency, or mixture models may further improve absolute fit.

5.4.1 A5.1 Exclusion of Sub-Chance Participants

To verify that the inclusion of 12 participants who performed at or below chance (\(\leq\) 55% accuracy) in some conditions did not bias our main findings, we conducted sensitivity analyses comparing the primary model (N=67) with models fit after excluding these participants (N=55).

Method: We refit the primary model (Model3_Difficulty) and an additive model (Model4_Additive) on the reduced dataset excluding sub-chance participants. Parameter estimates were compared using delta (sensitivity - baseline) with conservative 95% credible intervals. If the delta CI includes zero and the baseline and sensitivity CIs overlap, we conclude the parameter is robust to exclusion.

Results: Most key parameters showed robust estimates when excluding sub-chance participants. For Model3_Difficulty and Model4_Additive, the drift intercept and boundary separation showed delta CIs that included zero, indicating no meaningful change. Conclusion: Sensitivity analyses confirmed that inclusion of these participants did not alter the pattern of main effects. The inclusion of sub-chance participants did not meaningfully alter main effects or substantive conclusions, supporting our decision to retain all 67 participants to maximize sample size and leverage hierarchical modeling’s ability to stabilize estimates through shrinkage.

5.4.3 A5.3 Task Modality Effects: Sensitivity Analysis

The primary model (Table 8) excluded Task (ADT vs. VDT) as a predictor for drift rate (\(v\)) and boundary separation (\(a\)) to reduce model complexity.   • Drift rate (\(v\)): ~ difficulty + effort * task (with interaction)
  • Boundary separation (\(a\)): ~ difficulty + effort + task (main effects only)
  • All other parameters unchanged from the primary model

Key findings (when task-sensitivity model is available):

  1. Effort effect is robust: The Effort → Drift effect estimate changes minimally when Task is included, and the 95% CrI remains entirely negative, confirming that high effort reduces drift rate regardless of model specification.

  2. Task main effect: Task modality (ADT vs. VDT) may or may not reliably affect drift rate; the task-sensitivity model quantifies this.

  3. Effort × Task interaction: A negligible interaction indicates that effort affects both modalities similarly; a significant interaction would suggest modality-specific vulnerability to dual-task interference.

Conclusion: The core finding—that high effort reduces drift rate—is robust to Task specification. Including Task improves model flexibility but does not alter the substantive conclusion regarding cognitive-motor interference in aging.

5.4.4 A5.4 PPC Validation Method

Posterior Predictive Checks (PPC) were performed to validate model fit by comparing observed data to data simulated from the fitted model. To avoid aggregation bias (Jensen’s Inequality) inherent in using group-level mean parameters in non-linear formulas, we used full posterior predictive sampling that respects subject-level random effects (Vehtari et al., 2021a). This approach generates predictions for every trial in the dataset, maintaining the hierarchical structure of the model.

PPC Implementation: For the primary model, we generated 1,000 posterior predictive draws using brms::posterior_predict() with negative_rt = TRUE to obtain signed reaction times (positive RT = “Different”/upper boundary, negative RT = “Same”/lower boundary). This parameter is critical for correctly extracting choice predictions from brms Wiener models. For each draw, we computed the proportion of “Different” responses and compared the distribution of predicted proportions to the observed proportion in the data.

PPC Results: On Standard trials, the model accurately predicted choice proportions: observed 10.9% “Different” responses vs. predicted 11.2% (95% credible interval: [9.9%, 12.7%]). The difference of 0.3% falls well within acceptable ranges, confirming that the model captures the data distribution accurately. The observed value falls within the 95% credible interval, indicating excellent model fit. Choice-proportion PPC validation is shown in Figure 33.

5.4.8 A5.7 Parameter Consistency and Sanity Checks

To validate the internal consistency of our model estimates, we performed three sanity checks recommended by independent expert review. 5.4.9 RT Asymmetry on Standard Trials

On Standard trials, the model estimated a strong negative drift rate (v = -1.404) toward “Same” responses, combined with a slight starting-point bias toward “Different” (z = 0.567). To verify the consistency of these estimates, we examined whether RT patterns align with model predictions.

Result: “Same” responses were significantly faster than “Different” responses (mean RT: 1.03 s vs. 1.32 s, difference = 293 ms). This pattern aligns perfectly with the model’s prediction: strong negative drift causes rapid evidence accumulation toward “Same”, resulting in fast “Same” responses. The slower “Different” responses likely reflect rare errors that occur when the process fails to reach the “Same” boundary within the response window.

5.4.10 Hard Trial Drift Direction

The primary model estimated that Hard trials have negative drift relative to Standard trials. To verify this estimate is consistent with observed below-chance accuracy on Hard trials, we examined the posterior distribution of Hard trial drift rates.

Result: Hard trials show consistently negative drift (mean v = -0.643, 95% CrI: [-0.740, -0.546], P(v < 0) = 100%). This confirms that the sensory evidence for difference on Hard trials is too weak to overcome the baseline tendency toward “Same”, explaining the observed below-chance accuracy (~30%) on Hard trials.

5.4.11 Subject Heterogeneity in Drift Rates

The discrepancy between analytical predictions (using group-level mean parameters) and PPC results (using full posterior with subject heterogeneity) suggests substantial individual differences in drift rates. To verify this, we examined the distribution of subject-level drift rate estimates.

Result: Subject-level drift rates show substantial heterogeneity (SD = 0.65, range: -3.08 to -0.21). Most subjects (60%) show strong negative drift (|v| ≥ 1.0), while a small subset (4.5%) show weak drift (|v| < 0.5). This heterogeneity explains the PPC vs. analytical formula discrepancy: subjects with weaker drift contribute disproportionately to error rates, but their contribution is masked when using group-level mean parameters.

Conclusion: All three sanity checks confirm the internal consistency of our model estimates. RT patterns, drift directions, and individual differences align with model predictions, providing strong evidence that the hierarchical DDM accurately captures the decision-making processes in our data.
