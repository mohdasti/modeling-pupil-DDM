# Introduction

July lines 1077–1150

Older adults show pervasive response-time slowing across nearly all task domains, historically attributed to a generalized decline in processing speed [@birren1965age; @salthouse1985speed; @salthouse1996]. Much of this slowing is also strategic: older adults favor accuracy over speed, adopting a more cautious response mode to offset declining neural efficiency [@rabbitt1979old; @ratcliff2004aging; @starns2010]. These patterns are well documented in sedentary laboratory settings, yet everyday cognition often unfolds while people are simultaneously exerting physical effort.

For older adults the cost of such exertion is disproportionately high, given age-related declines in motor efficiency and reduced neural reserve [@verhaeghen2003]. Resource-competition accounts hold that maintaining a grip or posture draws on the same limited pool of resources needed for the decision itself [@wickens2008]. Physical load is known to impair performance [@verhaeghen2003; @woollacott2002], but it remains unclear whether it merely slows peripheral execution or degrades the quality of information processing. We address this question with a computational decomposition of decision-making under concurrent effort.

The drift-diffusion model (DDM) offers such a decomposition [@ratcliff1978theory]. It treats each decision as the gradual accumulation of noisy evidence over time, governed by a deterministic mean drift rate ($v$)—the quality of the sensory evidence—and a stochastic noise term ($\sigma$) reflecting moment-to-moment fluctuations in the nervous system. Evidence accumulates until it reaches one of two response boundaries (@fig-ddm-process, Panel B).


The model decomposes observed reaction times and accuracy into four latent parameters. Drift rate ($v$) indexes the efficiency and clarity of evidence accumulation, driving the decision toward the upper ('different') or lower ('same') boundary. Boundary separation ($a$) is the amount of evidence required before committing to a response—a direct measure of caution. Starting-point bias ($z$) captures any a priori preference for one response before the trial begins. Non-decision time ($t_0$) absorbs peripheral processes outside the decision itself, such as sensory encoding and motor execution [@ratcliff1978theory; @ratcliff2008diffusion; @voss2004interpreting].

This decomposition is especially valuable in aging, where behavioral summaries can mask the underlying changes. On simple perceptual tasks such as brightness or letter discrimination, older adults typically show elevated boundary separation and longer non-decision times while drift rate remains largely preserved [@ratcliff2001aging; @ratcliff2004aging; @ratcliff2008diffusion]. This preservation is fragile, however: as demands increase through perceptual load, visual search, or memory retrieval, the efficiency of evidence accumulation declines [@madden1991; @spaniol2006]. Whether these parameters reflect fixed capacity limits or are dynamically modulated by physiological state remains open—and manipulating systemic arousal through physical effort offers a direct test, since high effort might force a compensatory increase in boundary caution or instead degrade the efficiency of evidence accumulation.

Arousal and performance have long been described by an inverted-U function, in which intermediate arousal is optimal and both hypo- and hyper-arousal impair performance [@yerkes1908]. Adaptive Gain Theory recasts this relationship mechanistically, treating arousal as a modulator of neural gain that sets the signal-to-noise ratio of information processing [@astonjones2005]. In aging, the operating range of these autonomic systems narrows: shifts in baseline arousal and reduced physiological flexibility leave older adults prone to over-activation, so that modest increases in task demand can push arousal past its optimum into a supra-optimal zone where internal noise compromises stable processing [@huang2024; @mather2016; @mikneviciute2022].

Concurrent physical load is a potent somatic driver of arousal. Because older adults have reduced motor efficiency and physiological reserve, sustaining an isometric grip costs disproportionately more subjective effort than in younger adults, recruiting explicit top-down control for operations that were once automatic and turning the motor task into a competitor for central resources [@verhaeghen2003]. Older adults performing continuous tasks show exaggerated autonomic reactivity that predicts subsequent cognitive fatigue [@hess2012], and this somatic burden selectively impairs their accuracy under conditions that leave younger adults unaffected [@azer2023]. We therefore treat resource competition [@wickens2008] and arousal accounts as integrated rather than independent: the central resources needed to sustain a high-intensity grip push the aging nervous system into hyper-arousal, creating a bottleneck in which processing efficiency is both drained by capacity limits and obscured by arousal-induced noise. Gross behavioral measures cannot adjudicate between these mechanisms—a drop in accuracy could reflect degraded sensory processing or a deliberate lowering of the threshold to escape discomfort—but decomposing behavior within the diffusion framework distinguishes whether effort suppresses evidence quality or collapses decision boundaries.

To track the underlying physiological state, we use pupillometry as a continuous, non-invasive index of central arousal, as pupil diameter follows autonomic tone with high temporal precision [@beatty1982; @kahneman1966]. Baseline pupil diameter reflects slower, tonic shifts in alertness, whereas the task-evoked pupil response (TEPR) is a rapid, phasic signal that tracks moment-to-moment cognitive and somatic workload [@gilzenrat2010; @vangerven2004]. Quantifying the TEPR as a trial-wise area under the curve (AUC) lets us measure how the demand of sustaining a grip shifts physiological investment from one choice to the next. Pupil size is also sensitive to luminance, viewing distance, and motor reflexes [@beatty1982; @loewenfeld1999], so we run the paradigm in an MRI environment with constant background luminance and a fixed viewing distance, allowing transient TEPR changes to be read as a relative index of phasic arousal. Although animal and pharmacological work links phasic pupil dilation to locus coeruleus–norepinephrine activity [@gilzenrat2010; @joshi2016], we treat the TEPR as a functional index of arousal state, not an anatomical biomarker; whether concurrent motor demand complements or obscures these cognitively driven dilations remains open.

Entering this single-trial signal into the model improves on block-averaged DDM estimates, which ignore the continuous fluctuation of internal state within a session, by mapping phasic arousal directly onto specific latent decision processes under sustained load. A foundational demonstration came from Cavanagh and colleagues [-@cavanagh2014], who used single-trial pupillometry during probabilistic learning to show that transient pupil dilations predicted increased boundary separation ($a$) on high-conflict choices—suggesting that a surge in phasic arousal can signal the brain to adopt greater caution. We extend this approach from cognitive conflict to physical effort, and from young adults to an aging sample. Whereas conflict in young adults mainly modulates top-down thresholds, advanced age combined with concurrent exertion strains shared processing resources. By fitting pupil-informed models under varying physical load—with nested extensions on drift, starting-point bias, and boundary separation—we test whether trial-by-trial pupil-indexed arousal tracks strategic caution ($a$) or the efficiency of evidence accumulation ($v$).

We tested four predictions about how concurrent physical load modulates decision formation in older adults. First, we expected drift rate ($v$) to decrease under high effort (40% MVC) relative to low effort (5% MVC), as the burden of a high-intensity contraction drains shared central resources and pushes arousal past the Yerkes–Dodson optimum, degrading the efficiency of evidence accumulation. Second, we contrasted two accounts of boundary separation ($a$): an adaptive-caution account, in which hyper-arousal prompts a strategic widening of thresholds to protect performance, versus a somatic-fatigue account, in which physical discomfort drives a collapse of thresholds as participants try to end trials quickly. Third, we asked whether non-decision time ($t_0$) increases under high effort, which would indicate direct cognitive-motor interference; because the response-signal paradigm constrains estimation of non-decision processes, this was treated as exploratory rather than a primary hypothesis. Fourth, we tested whether the bias-attenuating effect of arousal extends from transient to sustained states: phasic arousal bursts are known to suppress choice biases, so if tonic somatic arousal behaves similarly, starting-point bias ($z$) should shift toward neutrality ($z = 0.5$) under high effort, whereas the chronic overhead of the motor task might instead entrench stable, energy-saving heuristics.

The present study applies hierarchical Bayesian drift-diffusion modeling to examine how physical effort (Low: 5% MVC vs. High: 40% MVC isometric handgrip) modulates latent decision parameters in older adults performing auditory and visual same–different discrimination tasks. Trial-by-trial task-evoked pupil responses (TEPR) provide an exploratory index of phasic arousal. Together, these analyses test whether the characteristic slowing and caution of older decision-makers reflect fixed capacity limits or physiological-state-dependent features modulated by effort-induced arousal.

## Participants

## Stimuli and Task Design

July lines 1476–1489

**Task overview** Participants completed two response-signal same/different discrimination tasks—an Auditory Detection Task (ADT) and a Visual Detection Task (VDT)—each performed under concurrent hand-grip effort manipulation. In both tasks, a standard and probe stimulus were presented in succession, and participants judged whether the stimuli were the same or different using button presses, followed by a confidence rating.

**Trial structure.** Both tasks shared the same response-signal timeline (see @fig-ddm-process, Panel A). Each trial began with a jittered blank interval (1.5–4.5 s) displaying only a gray background. Participants were then shown a 3-second rectangular grip gauge with a marker positioned at either 5% or 40% from the bottom, indicating the target force level to be maintained using a hand-held dynamometer (left hand) at the corresponding percentage of their maximum voluntary contraction (MVC). MVC was determined during a brief calibration procedure prior to each task. Following the grip period, a 0.25-second blank screen and 0.5-second fixation dot preceded stimulus presentation.

**Auditory Detection Task (ADT).** The standard stimulus was a 0.1-second sinusoidal tone at 1000 Hz. After a 0.5-second inter-stimulus interval, the probe stimulus was presented as a 0.1-second tone that was either identical to the standard ($\Delta frequency$ = 0) or positively offset by $\Delta frequency \in \{8, 16, 32, 64\}\,\mathrm{Hz}$. For drift-diffusion model (DDM) analyses, the four non-zero offsets were collapsed into two difficulty bins: Hard = {8, 16} Hz and Easy = {32, 64} Hz, with Standard = 0 Hz.

**Visual Detection Task (VDT).** The standard stimulus was a centrally presented sinusoidal Gabor patch (spatial frequency 1.5 cycles/degree, Michelson contrast 0.20, diameter 4°, duration 0.1 s). After a 0.5-second inter-stimulus interval, the probe stimulus was presented as a 0.1-second Gabor that was either identical to the standard ($\Delta contrast$ = 0) or showed a positive contrast increase of $\Delta contrast \in \{0.06, 0.12, 0.24, 0.48\}$, with all other parameters held constant. For DDM analyses, offsets were collapsed into Hard = {0.06, 0.12} and Easy = {0.24, 0.48}, with Standard = 0.

**Response collection.** Following stimulus offset, participants relaxed their grip and had a 3-second response window to indicate whether the two stimuli were the same or different using their right hand (index finger = "different"; middle finger = "same"). This was immediately followed by another 3-second window in which participants provided a confidence rating on a 4-point scale (1 = low confidence, 4 = high confidence).

**Trial counts and monitoring.** Each task comprised 150 trials distributed across five blocks of 30 trials each. Within each block, the five stimulus levels (Standard plus four offsets) were crossed with the two effort conditions (5% vs. 40% MVC) and presented in pseudorandom order with equal proportions (three repetitions per stimulus × effort combination). Hand-grip performance was monitored after each block, and participants received corrective feedback if they consistently failed to maintain the dynamometer at the designated force level (near the grip gauge marker) and/or for the correct duration (until offset of the probe stimulus).

## Data Processing and Quality Control

July lines 1494–1554

**Reaction time definition.** In this response-signal design, reaction time (RT) was initially recorded as the latency from response-screen onset (“Same or Different?” prompt) to button press, termed **cue-locked RT**. However, perceptual evidence becomes available at probe onset (the second stimulus). To align RT with evidence accumulation onset, we derived **probe-onset-locked RT** by adding the fixed post-probe delay to cue-locked RT:

$$RT_{\text{probe-onset}} = RT_{\text{cue-locked}} + 0.35\text{ s}.$$ {#eq-probe}

where 0.35 s comprises 0.10 s probe duration plus 0.25 s grip-relaxation period. Probe-onset-locked RT served as the primary timing measure for DDM interpretation, as it aligns the decision process with evidence onset. Under this convention, the DDM non-decision time parameter ($t_0$) includes the fixed 0.35 s response-gating delay plus residual encoding and motor components. Cue-locked RT was retained for sensitivity analyses. This probe-onset-locked timing is our chosen convention to align $t_0$ with evidence onset; some DDM studies alternatively use cue-locked or response-locked reference points depending on task structure.

**Gated response-signal validity.** Overt responses were withheld until the response screen (a fixed 0.35 s after probe offset), so cue-locked RT mixes pre-response processing with enforced waiting. Probe-onset locking shifts the accumulation clock to evidence onset while absorbing the fixed gating interval into $t_0$ (intercept-only in the primary model). This follows standard practice for response-signal and masked-response designs: condition contrasts are identified within a shared timing regime, and absolute $t_0$ estimates are not interpreted as pure motor time [@ratcliff2008diffusion; @starns2010]. We treat probe-onset locking as a conservative alignment choice; free-response replications without enforced waiting would be needed to isolate encoding-only $t_0$ components.

**Trial exclusion criteria.** To minimize the influence of premature or accidental button presses, trials with cue-locked RT < 0.20 s were excluded from the primary analysis (sensitivity analyses using alternative cutoffs of 0.15 s and 0.25 s are reported in the Supplementary Materials). This lower bound was applied to cue-locked RT before constructing probe-onset-locked RT for the primary DDM models. Additional exclusions included missed responses, trials from runs flagged for invalid performance (defined as run-level accuracy at or below chance, indicating the participant did not engage with the task), and trials with non-positive or missing RT values (i.e., recording artifacts where RT was logged as zero or a negative number). For pupil-linked DDM models, trials additionally required valid pupil data (see Pupillometry Preprocessing section).

## Statistical analysis

July lines 1555–1560

**Design.** The study employed a 2 (Task: ADT, VDT) × 3 (Difficulty: Standard, Easy, Hard) × 2 (Effort: Low, High) within-subjects factorial design, yielding 12 experimental conditions per participant. Because difficulty is determined by stimulus offset magnitude and thus unknown at trial onset, it was not included as a predictor of starting-point bias in the primary DDM specification.

**Modeling approach.** ADT and VDT were modeled *jointly* in a single hierarchical Bayesian DDM, with Task (ADT vs. VDT) included as a fixed effect on starting-point bias $z$. This joint specification estimates task-specific parameter offsets (i.e., how ADT and VDT differ in bias) while sharing subject-level random-effect variance across tasks, allowing hierarchical shrinkage to stabilize individual-level estimates. Importantly, drift rate $v$ and boundary separation $a$ were not modeled as functions of Task in the primary model — instead, task-specific estimates for these parameters are provided as a sensitivity analysis in Supplementary Section S4.3. This choice reflects our primary interest in the pooled effort and difficulty effects, with task-modality differences treated as secondary context.

### Analysis overview {#sec-stats-overview}

July lines 1561–1564

Analyses proceeded in three stages. First, **manipulation checks** confirmed that difficulty and effort affected behavioral performance (accuracy and RT) as intended, using generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMM, binomial family) for accuracy and linear mixed-effects models (LMM) for median RT via `lme4` [@bates2015lme4], each with fixed effects for Difficulty, Effort, and Task and random intercepts for participant; fixed-effects summaries used `broom.mixed` [@broommixed]. Full results are reported in Supplementary Section S7. Second, the **primary DDM analysis** used a hierarchical Bayesian Wiener diffusion model fit via `brms` [@burkner2017brms] in R [@R], with model comparison via Pareto-smoothed importance sampling LOO cross-validation (PSIS-LOO; [@vehtari2017loo]); full model specification is described in @sec-ddm-spec. Third, **pupil-linked DDM extensions** tested whether trial-wise task-evoked pupil responses (TEPR) modulated drift rate or response bias, using a nested sequence of pupil-augmented models compared via PSIS-LOO (@sec-pupil-ddm-models). Sensitivity analyses across RT cutoff thresholds are reported in Supplementary Section S4.2. **Exploratory individual-differences analyses** examined whether LC structural MRI metrics (Supplementary Section S8; [@bennett2024lc]) and neuropsychological test scores (Supplementary Section S9) related to DDM parameters or behavioral effort costs; these analyses were not used for model selection or primary hypothesis testing.

## Pupillometry Preprocessing and AUC Features {#sec-pupil-preprocessing}

July lines 1565–1580

**Recording and hardware.** Pupil diameter was continuously recorded using an MRI-compatible TRACKPixx binocular eye tracker (VPixx Technologies, Montreal, Canada) sampling at 2000 Hz. Raw eye-tracking data were exported as .mat files and processed through a multi-stage preprocessing pipeline designed to ensure high-quality pupillometry measurements.

**Artifact correction and quality control.** Blink artifacts and invalid samples were removed using the ET-remove-artifacts MATLAB toolbox (Huang et al., 2020), which employs a velocity-based detection algorithm configured with a 100th-order low-pass filter (passband: 10 Hz, stopband: 12 Hz) and peak/trough threshold factors of 5 standard deviations above baseline. Artifacts exceeding 2 seconds in duration were replaced with missing values rather than interpolated, following established methodological guidelines indicating that linear reconstruction over extended durations artificially compresses natural physiological variance and introduces spurious data trends ([@kret2019preprocessing; @mathot2018]). All recordings then underwent manual inspection using the toolbox's interactive plot editor to identify and correct any remaining undetected artifacts.

**Trial alignment and downsampling.** Cleaned pupil data were trial-aligned using event timestamps synchronized with behavioral event markers, then downsampled from 2000 Hz to 250 Hz using an 8th-order anti-aliasing filter to preserve signal quality while reducing computational demands. Baseline correction was applied by subtracting the mean pupil diameter during the baseline window (-0.5 s to 0 s relative to squeeze onset) from all timepoints in the trial.

**Decision-Response AUC specification and rationale.** We quantified trial-wise phasic cognitive workload by calculating the baseline-corrected pupil area under the curve (AUC) via trapezoidal integration. This metric, functionally designated as the Decision-Response AUC, was extracted from a 3.0-second window anchored directly to evidence onset, spanning from 0.3 to 3.3 seconds post-probe. This placement corresponds to 4.65 to 7.65 seconds relative to the initial handgrip squeeze, capturing the transient, stimulus-locked phasic arousal fluctuation riding on top of the sustained tonic effort baseline. Delaying the window onset by 300 milliseconds directly accounts for the inherent neurophysiological lag of the pupillary efferent pathway. The 3.0-second window duration is highly specialized. It encapsulates the complete morphological profile of the transient task-evoked pupil response (TEPR), which characteristically peaks at approximately 2.95 seconds post-stimulus in this cohort. Because the explicit response prompt appeared 0.35 seconds post-probe and mean cue-locked response latencies averaged 0.6 seconds, this expansive window spans both pre-decision evidence accumulation and post-decision motor and confidence processing. We directly isolated these overlapping post-decisional dynamics by entering reaction time as a covariate in all primary downstream pupil-informed models. We also defined a **Truncated Decision-Response AUC**—a 1.0-second integration window from 0.3 to 1.3 s post-probe (4.65 to 5.65 s relative to handgrip onset)—as a narrower alternative that minimizes post-response contamination at the cost of capturing less of the full TEPR morphology; the same nested pupil-DDM sequence was refit on this metric as a sensitivity analysis (@sec-pupil-ddm-integration).

Supplementary @suppfig-timing-supp shows the response-signal timing structure used in analyses.


**Confounds and sensitivity checks.** To minimize potential confounds from luminance and viewing distance, the paradigm was executed within a controlled MRI environment characterized by constant background luminance and a fixed viewing distance. Motor-related autonomic activity was addressed by including reaction time as a covariate in subsequent pupil-informed models.

## Computational model

July lines 1581–1584

The drift-diffusion framework and its four parameters ($v$, $a$, $z$, $t_0$) are introduced in the Introduction; this section describes the implementation-specific decisions for the present study.

### Decision coding and parameterization

July lines 1585–1590

**Response-side coding rationale.** We employed response-side coding (also termed stimulus coding) where the upper boundary maps to "different" choices and the lower boundary maps to "same" choices [@wiecki2013hddm; @ratcliff2008diffusion]. This configuration is mathematically required to independently isolate response-selection biases from true sensory discriminability (drift rate). Accuracy-based coding layouts (correct vs. incorrect boundaries) are structurally incapable of capturing these dynamics, as a bias parameter in an accuracy matrix merely reflects a trivial preference for being correct. Because individuals performing same–different discrimination paradigms frequently exhibit prominent response-specific heuristics (e.g., a conservative tendency to favor the "same" classification), anchoring boundaries directly to the physical response alternatives allows the model to estimate true pre-stimulus starting-point preferences. Under this parameterization, evidence accumulation directed toward a true "Standard" (same) stimulus is represented as a negative drift rate traveling toward the lower boundary, rather than a processing deficit.

**Implementation and model leverage.** Response-side coding was implemented by constructing a binary response vector, coded as 1 for "different" judgments and 0 for "same" judgments. Standard ($\Delta = 0$) trials provide critical mathematical leverage for identifying baseline starting-point bias ($z$) under this coding scheme. Because these baseline trials contain zero objective sensory evidence, they effectively isolate automatic response preferences from evidence accumulation speed. Full link function specifications and the underlying Wiener likelihood equations are detailed in Supplementary Section S6.

### Model specification: standard-only calibration and primary model {#sec-ddm-spec}

July lines 1591–1604

**Standard-only bias calibration model.** To structurally isolate baseline starting-point bias from active drift-rate estimation, we executed a two-stage modeling pipeline. The first stage fit a targeted calibration model restricted exclusively to Standard trials ($\approx$ 3,600 trials), which establishes an empirical baseline for choice preference in the complete absence of sensory evidence shifts. Starting-point bias ($z$) was allowed to vary as a function of task modality and effort condition—both of which were explicitly known to the participant pre-trial—but was constrained to be invariant across perceptual difficulty levels, as trial-wise difficulty was randomized and entirely unknown prior to evidence presentation. The full parameter estimates and descriptive levels resulting from this calibration step are detailed in Supplementary Section S2.

**Primary analysis model.** Our primary behavioral DDM integrated both testing domains by modeling the Auditory Discrimination Task (ADT) and Visual Discrimination Task (VDT) jointly within a single hierarchical Bayesian framework. This joint configuration enters task modality as a fixed effect on starting-point bias ($z$), allowing the model to isolate task-specific parameter offsets while sharing participant-level random-effect variance across sensory domains. This hierarchical design leverages partial pooling via shrinkage to stabilize individual parameter estimates and maximize statistical power for detecting our core effects of interest. 

Within this joint model architecture, drift rate ($v$) and boundary separation ($a$) were estimated as additive functions of perceptual task difficulty (treatment-coded, Standard as reference) and tonic effort condition (treatment-coded, Low as reference). Starting-point bias ($z$) was modeled as a function of task and effort, but remained excluded from difficulty subformulas because trial difficulty cannot be anticipated before evidence accumulation begins (see @fig-ddm-process, Panel C for a visual summary of predictor mappings). Non-decision time ($t_0$) was set to an intercept-only specification based on pilot identification constraints inherent to the gated response-signal design. Random intercepts by participant were comprehensively estimated for $v$, $a$, and $z$, while $t_0$ was retained as a fixed population parameter to ensure strict model identifiability. Task-separated parameter profiles are provided as an explicit sensitivity check in Supplementary Section S4.3 to validate that joint pooling did not obscure modality-specific trends.

**RT timing convention.** In this response-signal design, reaction time was defined as the latency from response-screen onset to the button press. However, because perceptual evidence becomes available immediately at probe onset, modeling distributions strictly from the execution cue presents a chronological misalignment between evidence availability and the mathematical integration start point. To ensure the diffusion window encompasses the entire information-processing timeline, probe-onset-locked RT was derived by adding the fixed 0.35-s post-probe delay (0.10-s probe duration plus 0.25-s grip-relaxation period) to the raw cue-locked value. 

Probe-onset-locked RT served as our primary timing convention because it correctly aligns the diffusion process with evidence onset (@suppfig-rt-distributions). Under this convention, the non-decision time parameter ($t_0$) absorbs the fixed 0.35-s response-gating delay alongside standard encoding and motor execution overhead [@ratcliff2007application; @voss2004interpreting]. While this constant addition can compress parameter variance in peripheral timing estimates, anchoring the model timeline to evidence onset is more theoretically appropriate for isolating accumulation dynamics under concurrent motor load. Raw cue-locked latency profiles and alternative lower-bound filtering cutoffs (0.15 s and 0.25 s) were retained for sensitivity analyses, which are reported in Supplementary Section S4.

**Behavioral validation checks.** To confirm that our perceptual difficulty and physical effort manipulations systematically altered gross behavioral performance independent of DDM assumptions, we conducted baseline mixed-effects model checks restricted to active discrimination trials (Easy and Hard conditions). Perceptual accuracy was evaluated using a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM, binomial logistic link), and median reaction times were evaluated using a linear mixed model (LMM). Both validation frameworks entered task modality, perceptual difficulty, and physical effort as fixed effects, alongside random participant intercepts. Full model parameters and frequentist significance metrics for these baseline checks are reported transparently in Supplementary Section S7.

### Pupil-Linked DDM Extensions {#sec-pupil-ddm-models}

July lines 1605–1612

Building upon the baseline behavioral model, we implemented hierarchical, pupil-informed regression extensions to evaluate whether within-person physiological fluctuations modulate parameters of choice on a trial-by-trial basis [@wiecki2013hddm]. The physical metric used for these extensions was the baseline-corrected Decision-Response AUC. To isolate true intra-individual variation from stable, between-participant differences in eye morphology, these trial-wise integration values were z-scored individually within each participant's complete timecourse profile. This localized transformation yielded a standardized single-trial metric that serves as our functional physiological proxy for transient arousal fluctuations during the decision window. 

To explicitly adjudicate between alternative mechanisms of state-dependent cognitive control, we evaluated a nested sequence of model configurations [@de2017dynamic]. The baseline behavioral model excluded all trial-level physiological inputs, establishing a structural reference point where parameter variance was governed solely by categorical task conditions. A bias-only model allowed this standardized pupil metric to modulate starting-point bias ($z$) through a logistic link function, testing the hypothesis that momentary arousal suppresses pre-existing response preferences and drives the initial decision architecture toward neutrality ($z = 0.5$). Finally, a dual-dependency model allowed the pupil metric to simultaneously modulate both bias and drift rate ($v$), testing whether momentary arousal additionally alters the efficiency of central sensory processing under a sustained motor load. We compared the relative predictive success of these architectures using Pareto-smoothed importance sampling leave-one-out cross-validation (PSIS-LOO; @vehtari2017loo), focusing population-level inference on the posterior regression coefficients ($\beta$) estimated on the link scale.

**Pupil × Perceptual Difficulty interaction model.** To evaluate the classic Yerkes–Dodson prediction that the effects of arousal on decision-making diverge as a function of immediate cognitive demands—with the optimal arousal threshold shifting according to task complexity—we implemented an interaction model crossing the standardized pupil metric with perceptual task difficulty. This specification directly evaluates the integrated resource-arousal framework established in our introduction, examining whether within-person fluctuations in phasic arousal differentially modulate parameter dynamics across sensory difficulty levels. In the primary nested sequence, pupil was evaluated on starting-point bias ($z$), drift + bias ($v$, $z$), and pupil × difficulty on drift (Models 1–3). To test the Cavanagh [-@cavanagh2014] boundary account directly, we additionally fit a Cavanagh-style extension allowing pupil to modulate boundary separation ($a$) only, compared to the same-$N$ behavioral baseline via PSIS-LOO (Decision-Response and Truncated Decision-Response AUC windows; @sec-pupil-ddm-integration, Supplementary Section S5). Non-decision time remained intercept-only.

### Priors, computation, and diagnostics {#sec-ddm-priors}

July lines 1613–3560

Priors were weakly informative and specified on the link scale. The boundary separation intercept used $\mathcal{N}(\log 1.3,\; 0.25)$ (corresponding to $a \approx 1.3$ at baseline), the non-decision time intercept used $\mathcal{N}(\log 0.35,\; 0.15)$ ($t_0 \approx 0.35$ s, probe-onset-locked), the drift rate intercept used $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, and the starting-point bias intercept used $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.5)$ ($z \approx 0.5$, no bias). # Discussion

July lines 4666–4706

Concurrent physical effort selectively degraded the quality of evidence accumulation in older adults. High effort reduced drift rate, consistent with resource competition or supra-optimal arousal (H1; @sec-key-contrasts), while leaving the other decision components largely unchanged. The boundary-separation contrast was directionally positive (H2; `r h2_beta_str`, `r h2_cri_str``r h2_p0_str`) but `r if (h2_rope_practically_null) "fell entirely within the project ROPE, so we do not interpret it as meaningful strategic caution" else "small relative to difficulty-driven variation in $a$"`, and starting-point bias showed a near-zero, uncertain effort contrast with no credible evidence of a shift toward neutrality (H4; @sec-starting-point-bias). Non-decision time could not be tested directly, because the response-signal design required an intercept-only $t_0$ (H3; see Limitations and future directions). The behavioral signature reinforces this localization: High effort reduced **accuracy** (manipulation check: $\beta = -0.16$, $p < .001$) without reliably slowing **reaction time** ($\beta$ = `r rt_effort_beta_str`, 95% CI `r rt_effort_ci_str`). The diffusion decomposition explains this dissociation — lower accuracy without RT slowing follows from a decline in drift rate (evidence quality) alongside a negligible change in boundary separation. The effect of effort on drift held when task was added as a predictor of drift, although task itself modulated absolute drift (@sec-key-contrasts, Supplementary Section~S4.3). `r pupil_xref_discussion` Overall, concurrent physical effort degraded evidence accumulation quality while producing only a negligible boundary adjustment, rather than a large strategic reconfiguration of decision thresholds or pre-stimulus bias.

The reduction in drift rate under High effort (40% MVC) relative to Low effort is the central finding and directly supports H1. Interpreted through Adaptive Gain Theory [@astonjones2005], this pattern is consistent with supra-optimal arousal under sustained grip: noise in neural representations degrades the signal-to-noise ratio driving evidence accumulation [@mather2016; @mikneviciute2022]. The diffusion model localizes the primary cost to evidence quality ($v$), with little accompanying change in caution or bias.

The boundary-separation contrast (H2) tells a different story. It is directionally positive (`r h2_beta_str`, `r h2_cri_str``r h2_p0_str`) but `r if (h2_rope_practically_null) sprintf("100\\%% of the posterior mass lies within the \\pm0.05 log-scale project ROPE, so we treat it as practically equivalent to zero rather than a compensatory caution adjustment") else "small relative to difficulty-driven variation"`. In natural-scale terms, the posterior median corresponds to `r h2_natural_pct_str` of baseline boundary separation ($\hat{a} \approx$ `r a_standard` in the Standard, Low-effort condition; @supptbl-ddm-posterior-estimates) — much smaller than routine variation in $a$ across difficulty levels ($\hat{a}$ ranging from `r a_hard` to `r a_standard`, Low effort). Empirical support for criterion widening under arousal comes from @cavanagh2014, who showed that pupil-indexed arousal during high-conflict choices predicted increased boundary separation — though that manipulation targeted transient cognitive conflict rather than sustained physical effort.

Notably, boundary estimates varied substantially by stimulus-driven difficulty (Easy: `r a_easy`, Standard: `r a_standard`, Hard: `r a_hard`; Low-effort posterior medians; @supptbl-ddm-posterior-estimates), confirming that the model is sensitive to caution adjustments driven by bottom-up perceptual demands. Future work using free-response paradigms (without response cues) could better isolate strategic flexibility from design-imposed temporal constraints and provide a more powerful test of effort-related criterion adjustment at 40% MVC.

A potential concern with response-side coding is whether the Wiener DDM misinterprets Standard trial responses — where the correct answer is 'Same' (lower boundary) — as errors, because they accumulate toward a negative drift (lower boundary rather than upper). This concern does not apply in the present implementation. The decresponse argument in brms encodes the observed response (Different = 1, Same = 0) as the boundary indicator, not a correctness flag. The model observes the actual RT and response, infers the latent evidence trajectory that would produce that RT–accuracy combination given the full design, and estimates parameters that best explain the joint RT × accuracy distribution across all conditions simultaneously. Accordingly, the strong negative drift on Standard trials (`r v_standard_str`) does not indicate that the model treats these as error trials; it correctly captures the fact that participants accurately pressed 'Same' by accumulating evidence toward the lower boundary. The fact that boundary separation is higher on Standard trials ($\hat{a} = `r a_standard`$, Low effort) than on Hard ($\hat{a} = `r a_hard`$) or Easy ($\hat{a} = `r a_easy`$) further validates this interpretation: the model responds to the exhaustive search demands of Standard trials by estimating a wider, more cautious boundary — the theoretically correct direction — rather than collapsing the boundary as it would if it incorrectly coded these as high-error conditions.

A related modeling choice deserves comment, because it can look asymmetric. Starting-point bias $z$ was constrained to be constant across difficulty levels, reflecting the logical impossibility of pre-trial criterion adjustment when trial difficulty is randomized and unknown prior to stimulus onset. In contrast, boundary separation $a$ was estimated as a function of difficulty and varied systematically (Standard: $\hat{a}$ = `r a_standard`; Hard: $\hat{a}$ = `r a_hard`; Easy: $\hat{a}$ = `r a_easy`; Low-effort medians; @supptbl-ddm-posterior-estimates).

This is not a contradiction. The two parameters reflect adjustments occurring at different timescales. Starting-point bias $z$ represents a true pre-stimulus strategic choice — where participants position themselves before evidence accumulation begins — and can only be set based on information available before the trial (task modality, effort condition). Boundary separation $a$, by contrast, is not a pre-trial strategic decision but rather an effective decision threshold inferred by the model from the shape of the empirical RT distribution within each condition. Participants do not "set" this boundary in advance; rather, the model recovers it as the latent parameter that best explains the observed RT variance under diffusion process assumptions [@ratcliff1985; @voss2004interpreting]. Over many trials, difficulty-dependent differences in RT dispersion and accuracy produce systematically different $a$ estimates even though individual participants are unaware of upcoming difficulty.

The larger $a$ on Standard trials reflects the model's accommodation of the characteristically broader, more right-skewed RT distribution observed on those trials — a distributional signature of the exhaustive comparison process required to confirm perfect sameness [@farell1985; @voss2004interpreting]. In this sense, $a$ captures the mechanistic consequence of within-trial processing demands, while $z$ captures a pre-trial strategic bias. Both are legitimate sources of condition-specific variation, but only $z$ requires advance knowledge of the trial type.

In interpreting the TEPR as a relative index of phasic arousal, we acknowledge measurement constraints under sustained grip (@sec-pupil-ddm-integration, @supptbl-tepr).


Turning from the accumulation process to its starting conditions, the near-zero effort contrast on starting-point bias (H4; `r h4_beta_str`, `r h4_cri_str``r h4_p0_str`) provides no credible evidence that concurrent handgrip at 40% MVC shifted pre-existing response preferences in older adults — though the credible interval does not formally exclude a small effect, and no equivalence threshold was established. A key interpretive consideration is the distinction between tonic and phasic arousal: the @degee2020pupil bias-resetting effect was demonstrated using phasic, within-trial arousal fluctuations indexed by pupil dilation, whereas the present H4 test relied on a sustained tonic effort manipulation. Tonic arousal elevation may not produce the same criterion-resetting dynamics as a phasic arousal event — a distinction that future work pairing trial-level TEPR with starting-point bias estimates could address directly. In the standard-only calibration model, starting-point bias was consistently above 0.5 ($z = `r z_overall_mean`$, 95% CrI [`r z_overall_q2.5`, `r z_overall_q97.5`]; @supptbl-standard-only-bias-levels), indicating a latent tendency to initiate accumulation closer to the "Different" boundary.

This latent starting-point offset should not be read directly as a liberal behavioral criterion in signal-detection terms. Manipulation checks showed near-ceiling accuracy on Standard (same) trials but poor accuracy on Hard trials (~30%; Supplementary Section S7) — a pattern dominated by **misses** (failure to report "different" when stimuli differed), not false alarms on same trials. The strongly negative drift on Standard trials (`r v_standard_str`) and the joint estimation of $v$ and $z$ across difficulty levels mean that a $z > 0.5$ starting point coexists with accurate "Same" responding rather than excess "Different" responses. The offset is therefore better interpreted as a DDM starting-point parameter under response-side coding — potentially reflecting a "Different-as-default" processing heuristic [@farell1985; @nosofsky1997palmeri] — than as a liberal SDT criterion producing elevated false-alarm rates.

Hard-trial accuracy at or below chance for many participants also indicates that some Hard offsets sat near perceptual threshold, weakening the assumption of a clean three-level difficulty gradient; this is noted in Limitations below. The absence of a credible effort contrast on $z$ is consistent with a stable task strategy rather than momentary arousal-driven criterion resetting, though this remains a graded null.

The absence of a detectable phasic bias-resetting effect stands in contrast to prior work in younger adults [@degee2020pupil] and may reflect age-related attenuation of phasic LC-NE signals [@huang2024]. `r pupil_xref_discussion` In the primary model, VDT showed higher starting-point bias toward "different" than ADT (`r h4task_primary_beta_str`, `r h4task_primary_cri_str``r h4task_primary_p0_str`; logit task offset), whereas the standard-only calibration model shows the opposite ordering (`r h4task_stdonly_beta_str`, `r h4task_stdonly_cri_str``r h4task_stdonly_p0_str`), illustrating the $v$–$z$ trade-off when drift is not held constant across difficulty. Exploratory LC-MRI correlates (Supplementary Section S8) did not link LC integrity to task-specific bias or to the group-level effort effect on drift, though those tests were underpowered.

Effort might also have acted more slowly, by reshaping decisions across trials rather than within them, but the temporal-dynamics analyses give little support for this. Exploratory choice-history models (Supplementary Section S10.1) showed a small but reliable perseveration tendency: pre-stimulus bias shifted by approximately 0.065 logit units toward the previous response, with the 95% CrI excluding zero. High effort (40% MVC) did not amplify this effect (previous-trial response × effort interaction $\hat{\beta}$ = −0.001, 95% CrI [−0.065, 0.064]). Together with the ROPE-negligible H2 boundary effect and the H4 bias null, this pattern suggests that effort degrades evidence quality upstream while producing only limited downstream adjustment in criterion-setting or history-driven responding [@wickens2008; @fischer2014serial].

Within-session fatigue models (Supplementary Section S10.2) showed a complementary pattern. Boundary separation relaxed slightly from the first to the second half of each run (more pronounced under Low effort), but drift rate did not decline credibly within runs, and age did not moderate the trajectory. The primary within-run dynamic is modest criterion adjustment, not accumulation-stage exhaustion [@drugowitsch2012; @tajima2016]. Effort's between-condition effect remains localized to drift rate ($v$), not to the temporal dynamics of boundary separation under load.

## Limitations and future directions

July lines 4707–4723

Several limitations constrain the interpretation of these findings. First, the standard Wiener DDM assumes constant drift within each trial and does not include across-trial variability in drift ($s_v$), starting point ($s_z$), or non-decision time ($s_{t_0}$). Second, the response-signal design enforced a fixed delay before overt responding and so required an intercept-only $t_0$; this means H3 could not be tested in the primary model, and probe-onset RT alignment identifies condition effects only within that shared timing regime rather than supporting strong claims about pure encoding or motor $t_0$ in isolation (@sec-ddm-spec). An exploratory effort effect on $\log t_0$ was small and imprecise ($\hat{\beta}$ = 0.02, 95% CrI [−0.05, 0.09]), precluding firm conclusions about peripheral slowing, and the accuracy decline under High effort aligns more directly with reduced drift rate than with a shift in $t_0$ (@supptbl-accuracy-glmm). Because reaction times are measured from response-screen onset rather than stimulus onset, the interpretation of $t_0$ is confined to motor execution and response selection rather than early perceptual encoding. These design choices were necessary given the MRI-compatible grip-force paradigm but limit generalizability to free-response paradigms where $t_0$ reflects the full encoding-decision-motor chain. Posterior predictive checks flagged all `r if (!is.na(ppc_n_cells_inline)) as.integer(ppc_n_cells_inline) else 12` experimental cells against pre-specified KS/QP thresholds (@supptbl-convergence-ppc-gate-appendix), with deviations concentrated in fast RT tails (worst in Easy/VDT). Mid-body quantile–probability alignment was nevertheless acceptable (Supplementary Section S3, @supptbl-ppc-midbody), and key effort contrasts were stable across alternative RT lower-bound thresholds (Supplementary Section S4.2, @supptbl-h1-h2-by-cutoff). Future extensions incorporating across-trial variability, a fast-guess contaminant mixture, or urgency-collapsing boundaries may improve absolute fit [@ratcliff2008diffusion].

`r pupil_xref_limitations`

Fourth, Hard-trial accuracy fell near or below chance for many participants (Supplementary Section S7), indicating that some Hard offsets sat near perceptual threshold rather than forming a clean graded difficulty manipulation above chance. Parameter estimates for Hard conditions should be interpreted with this floor effect in mind. Complementary sensitivity analyses for task-on-drift pooling and related robustness checks are reported in Supplementary Section S4 (@supptbl-task-sensitivity, @supptbl-h1-h2-by-cutoff) where already completed; a Standard+Easy-only primary refit excluding Hard trials remains planned.

Fifth, the concurrent isometric handgrip manipulation (40% MVC) may interact with motor execution in ways not fully captured by small fixed effects on $t_0$; future work integrating EMG or kinematic measures could disentangle central resource competition from peripheral motor interference. Linear Ballistic Accumulator or race models may also better accommodate the fast-tail RT dynamics observed in Easy-difficulty conditions, and future work could explore whether these model families yield the same substantive pattern of effort and difficulty effects on decision parameters.

Sixth, exploratory LC-MRI and neuropsychological correlates (Supplementary Sections S8 and S9) were underpowered for individual-differences moderation and did not enter the primary inferential framework; they are reported as descriptive context for sample characterization and do not alter the group-level DDM conclusions below.


Taken together, concurrent 40% MVC isometric grip degraded perceptual evidence accumulation in older adults more than it reorganized decision thresholds or pre-response bias. The hierarchical Wiener DDM localized the primary cost to drift rate $v$ — the signal-to-noise ratio of evidence integration — with a credible interval excluding zero, while the boundary and bias effects were practically negligible and the choice-history and within-run dynamics showed only minor adjustment. Non-decision time remained untestable in the primary model, and its exploratory estimate was small and imprecise. `r pupil_xref_conclusions`

Exploratory individual-differences analyses in the same cohort did not alter this group-level story. LC-MRI correlates (Supplementary Section S8; [@bennett2024lc]) were more closely related to between-person variability in sensitivity and baseline boundary settings than to mean perceptual sensitivity, trial-wise TEPR, or the effort effect on drift, and extended neuropsychological characterization (Supplementary Section S9) did not indicate that global cognition explained the effort effect on drift. Under concurrent physical load, then, older adults mainly accumulate less evidence per unit time, with only limited adjustment in caution or bias.
