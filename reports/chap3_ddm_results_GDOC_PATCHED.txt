Abstract
Sixty-seven older adults completed auditory and visual same–different judgments under low (5% MVC) or high (40% MVC) isometric grip while pupil diameter was recorded. A hierarchical Wiener DDM was fit with probe-onset-locked RT. Difficulty entered drift () and boundary separation (); task and effort entered starting-point bias (), held constant across difficulty because trials were randomized.
High effort lowered drift relative to low (, 95% CrI ; ; 4% ROPE mass). High effort produced a directionally positive boundary-separation contrast that was practically negligible under the project ROPE (, 95% CrI ; ). Effort did not credibly change bias (, 95% CrI ; ). Non-decision time () was group-level only in the primary model, so effort on peripheral timing was not tested there; an exploratory log- effort term was small and wide (, 95% CrI [−0.05, 0.09]).
Exploratory pupil-linked models showed near-zero TEPR associations with drift and bias, no predictive gain over the behavioral baseline on the pupil subset, and no LOO advantage for Cavanagh-style pupil→boundary extensions (Section 3.3). A pupil × difficulty interaction on drift did not support demand-dependent arousal effects (Section 3.3).
Primary contrasts held under alternative RT cutoffs and when task modality was included as a sensitivity check. Under concurrent grip, older adults mainly accumulated less evidence per unit time; caution and pre-response bias changed little.

1. Introduction
1.1 Physical Effort and Performance in the Aging Brain
Older adults show pervasive response time slowing across virtually all task domains, a finding historically attributed to generalized processing speed decline (Birren, 1965; Salthouse, 1985; Salthouse, 1996). Beyond simple biological slowing, this behavior is often strategic; older adults systematically favor accuracy over speed, adopting a more cautious response mode to compensate for declining neural efficiency (Rabbitt, 1979; Ratcliff et al., 2004; Starns & Ratcliff, 2010). While these patterns are well-documented in sedentary laboratory settings, real-world cognitive tasks frequently occur while simultaneously exerting physical effort.
For older adults, the “cost” of such exertion is significantly higher due to age-related declines in motor efficiency and reduced neural reserves (Verhaeghen et al., 2003). According to resource competition theories, the executive demands required to maintain a physical grip or posture compete for the same limited pool of cognitive resources needed for decision-making (Wickens, 2008). While it is established that physical load can impair general performance (Verhaeghen et al., 2003; Woollacott & Shumway-Cook, 2002), we do not yet know whether this interference simply slows down peripheral execution or fundamentally degrades the quality of information processing. This study utilizes a computational framework to decompose these effects, investigating how physical effort and physiological state interact to shape decision-making in the aging brain.
1.2 Decomposing Decisions: The Drift-Diffusion Model
To understand the latent mechanisms underlying age-related slowing, we utilize the Drift-Diffusion Model (Ratcliff, 1978). This computational framework assumes that decision-making is a continuous process of evidence accumulation over time, driven by two primary opposing forces that define the internal dynamics of the choice. The first of these internal forces is the deterministic mean drift rate (), which represents the true quality or strength of the sensory evidence extracted from a stimulus. The second force is the stochastic diffusion constant (), capturing the inherent noise and moment-to-moment Gaussian fluctuations in the nervous system. Together, these two forces determine the trajectory of a decision vector over time until it intersects a boundary, a dynamic process visually formalized in Figure 1 (Panel B). As shown in this architectural layout, the final choice and its corresponding timing are ultimately governed by the decision architecture, which dictates the structural boundaries and thresholds within which this continuous evidence accumulation occurs.

	This mathematical framework allows us to decompose standard behavioral reaction time data and accuracy rates into four distinct latent parameters. The drift rate () reflects the efficiency and quality of the accumulation process itself, where the absolute magnitude of the parameter reflects the clarity of the sensory signal and the efficiency of the accumulation process itself, directing the decision vector toward either the upper (‘different’) or lower (‘same’) boundary based on stimulus properties. The boundary separation () establishes the total amount of evidence required before a participant commits to a response, serving as a direct measure of response caution. The starting-point bias () accounts for any a priori preference or baseline asymmetry toward one response alternative over another before the trial even begins. Finally, the non-decision time () captures the duration of peripheral mechanisms that fall completely outside the core decision process, such as initial sensory encoding and downstream motor execution (Ratcliff, 1978; Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008; Voss et al., 2004).
Decomposing these metrics is critical when examining an aging population because standard behavioral observations often mask the true underlying cognitive changes. Previous research demonstrates a clear processing dissociation in older adults. When performing simple perceptual tasks like brightness or letter discrimination, older adults consistently exhibit elevated boundary separation and longer non-decision times, yet their underlying drift rate remains remarkably preserved (Ratcliff et al., 2001, 2004; Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008). However, this preservation is fragile. When task demands intensify through increased perceptual load, complex visual search, or episodic memory retrieval, the efficiency of evidence accumulation begins to decline significantly (Madden & Allen, 1991; Spaniol et al., 2006).
An open question is whether these DDM parameters represent rigid capacity limits or if they are dynamically modulated by an individual’s immediate physiological state. By using physical effort to manipulate systemic arousal, we can test whether these internal mechanisms are flexible under stress. Specifically, we can determine if the increased physiological cost of high physical effort forces an alteration in the decision architecture, perhaps prompting a compensatory increase in boundary caution, or if it directly degrades the efficiency of the evidence accumulation process itself. This methodology allows us to move past the simple observation that older adults slow down under pressure, revealing instead the real-time interaction between somatic demand and cognitive efficiency.
1.3 Arousal, Effort, and Decision Performance in Older Adults
The relationship between physiological arousal and cognitive performance has long been conceptualized as an inverted-U function, where intermediate states yield optimal execution while hypo- or hyper-arousal leads to systemic performance degradation (Yerkes & Dodson, 1908). Adaptive Gain Theory provides a functional account of this classic relationship by frameworking arousal as a dynamic modulator of neural gain, which directly dictates the signal-to-noise ratio of information processing during task-relevant behavior (Aston-Jones & Cohen, 2005). When applied to the psychology of aging, empirical evidence suggests that the dynamic range of these autonomic regulation systems becomes significantly constricted. Age-related shifts in baseline arousal settings and diminished physiological flexibility make older adults highly vulnerable to over-activation. Consequently, modest increases in objective task demands can rapidly elevate systemic arousal past the optimal peak and into a supra-optimal zone where excessive internal noise compromises cognitive stability (Huang & Clewett, 2024; Mather & Harley, 2016; Mikneviciute et al., 2022).
This physiological vulnerability becomes highly salient when cognitive operations are performed under concurrent physical load, which acts as a powerful somatic driver of systemic arousal. Because older adults exhibit reduced motor efficiency and diminished physiological reserves, maintaining an isometric grip demands a vastly disproportionate expenditure of subjective effort relative to younger cohorts. This cognitive-motor cost reflects an age-related shift wherein previously automated sensorimotor operations must recruit explicit, top-down executive control, effectively transforming a somatic task into a direct competitor for limited central processing resources (Verhaeghen et al., 2003). Older adults performing continuous processing tasks exhibit exaggerated autonomic reactivity, measured via prominent shifts in systolic blood pressure, which directly predict heightened states of subsequent cognitive fatigue (Hess & Ennis, 2012). Crucially, this somatic burden directly interferes with simultaneous processing, selectively impairing performance accuracy in older adults under conditions where younger adults remain entirely unaffected (Azer et al., 2023). Rather than treating resource competition (Wickens, 2008) and arousal-based models as independent accounts, we argue they are functionally integrated. The substantial central resources required to sustain a high-intensity physical grip force the aging nervous system into a state of hyper-arousal. This sustained somatic load pushes the organism past the optimal peak of the functional gain curve, generating a resource bottleneck where cognitive processing efficiency is simultaneously drained by structural capacity limits and obscured by hyper-arousal-induced noise.
Although it is clear that concurrent physical effort alters gross behavioral metrics like accuracy, traditional performance measures are fundamentally blind to the underlying latent mechanisms driving these failures. For example, a drop in accuracy under high physical load could reflect a degradation in the quality of sensory processing, but it could alternatively signal a top-down strategic shift where a participant deliberately lowers their decision threshold to escape the discomfort of sustained muscular contraction. Traditional reaction time and error analyses cannot adjudicate between these competing explanations. By decomposing behavioral data into distinct computational parameters within a diffusion framework, we can explicitly isolate these hidden components. Identifying whether physical effort selectively suppresses evidence quality or collapses decision boundaries provides critical insight into the functional architecture of cognitive-motor interference. This computational approach moves the field beyond merely documenting that physical demand impairs cognition, providing instead a clear mechanistic account of how somatic load shapes the speed, accuracy, and strategic boundaries of human choice.
1.4 Pupillometry and Pupil-Informed Drift-Diffusion Modeling
To empirically capture these shifting states of resource allocation, we utilize pupillometry as a continuous physiological proxy for an individual’s internal state. Non-invasive eye tracking provides a highly sensitive measure of central nervous system activation, where fluctuations in pupil diameter track changes in autonomic tone with exceptional temporal precision (Beatty, 1982; Kahneman & Beatty, 1966). Within this methodology, two distinct components offer insight into a participant’s physiological state. While baseline pupil diameter reflects slower shifts in tonic arousal and sustained alertness, the task-evoked pupil response (TEPR) acts as a rapid, phasic signal that directly tracks moment-to-moment changes in cognitive and somatic workload (Gilzenrat et al., 2010; Van Gerven et al., 2004). Quantifying this phasic response via the trial-wise area under the curve (AUC) allows us to measure how the immediate demand of sustaining a physical grip alters an individual’s physiological investment from one choice to the next.
Of course, peripheral pupil metrics are not driven solely by internal arousal; they are inherently sensitive to extraneous variables such as ambient luminance, viewing distance, and direct motor execution reflexes (Beatty, 1982; Loewenfeld & Lowenstein, 1999). We rigorously control for these potential confounds by executing our paradigm within a highly regulated MRI environment characterized by constant background luminance and a locked viewing distance. Under these invariant conditions, transient changes in the TEPR can be confidently interpreted as a relative index of phasic arousal rather than a sensory or physical artifact. Furthermore, while a wealth of animal and neuropharmacological research firmly links transient pupil dilations to central locus coeruleus-norepinephrine activity (Gilzenrat et al., 2010; Joshi et al., 2016), our psychophysiological approach treats the TEPR strictly as a functional, systemic index of phasic arousal state rather than a direct anatomical biomarker of midbrain nuclei integrity. However, an open physiological question remains whether the concurrent somatic demands of a sustained motor action complement or structurally obscure these transient, cognitively driven pupillary dilations under high workload.
Integrating this single-trial psychophysiological metric directly into a computational model offers a substantial analytical advantage over traditional behavioral modeling. Standard applications of the drift-diffusion model generate static parameter estimates averaged across entire blocks of trials. This aggregate approach completely ignores the reality that a participant’s internal state fluctuations occur continuously throughout a session. By incorporating trial-by-trial pupil data directly into the hierarchical DDM framework, we can map these transient, phasic arousal fluctuations onto specific latent decision processes, revealing how moment-to-moment cognitive preparation shifts under the broader constraints of a sustained physical load.
A foundational demonstration of this technique was provided by Cavanagh and colleagues (2014), who utilized single-trial pupillometry during a probabilistic learning task to show that transient pupil dilations specifically predicted an increase in boundary separation () during high-conflict choices. This finding suggested that under cognitive conflict, a sudden surge in phasic arousal acts as a signaling mechanism that prompts the brain to adopt greater response caution.
Our study extends this methodology by shifting the experimental focus from cognitive conflict to physical effort, and from healthy young cohorts to an aging population. This shift provides a fundamentally distinct test of how internal arousal states govern decision architecture. While cognitive conflict in young adults primarily modulates top-down strategic thresholds, the combination of advanced age and concurrent physical exertion introduces a severe strain on shared processing resources. Consequently, by testing pupil-informed models under varying physical loads—including nested extensions on drift, starting-point bias, and boundary separation—we evaluated whether trial-by-trial fluctuations in pupil-indexed phasic arousal track strategic caution () or degradation in evidence accumulation () under sustained physical load.
1.5 Hypotheses and Predictions
We evaluated four primary predictions regarding how concurrent physical load modulates the latent components of decision formation in older adults. First, we hypothesized that the drift rate () would significantly decrease under the high-effort condition (40% MVC) relative to the low-effort baseline (5% MVC). This prediction directly builds upon the principles of resource competition and functional arousal dynamics established across the preceding sections. The severe somatic burden of maintaining a high-intensity muscular contraction is expected to force the aging nervous system past the optimal peak of the Yerkes-Dodson curve, draining shared central resources and ultimately degrading the clarity and efficiency of sensory evidence accumulation.
Second, we evaluated two competing accounts regarding boundary separation (). Under an adaptive caution account extrapolated from cognitive conflict frameworks, systemic hyper-arousal might prompt a top-down, strategic widening of thresholds to protect performance stability under strain. Conversely, under a somatic fatigue account, the acute physical discomfort of sustained muscular contraction could drive a collapse of boundaries as participants attempt to terminate trials quickly. Adjudicating between these accounts allows us to determine whether older adults dynamically recalibrate their decision criteria under tonic somatic load.
Third, we addressed the impact of motor demand on peripheral overhead by exploring whether non-decision time () would increase under high physical effort, a shift that would reflect direct cognitive-motor interference during concurrent grip maintenance. Because the inherent structural constraints of the response-signal paradigm restrict full estimation flexibility for non-decision processes, this evaluation was treated as an explicit exploratory analysis rather than a primary structural hypothesis. These parameter variations are reported transparently to provide a complete overview of the model space despite these paradigm-specific mathematical limitations.
Fourth, we tested whether the bias-attenuating properties of physiological arousal generalize from transient states to sustained states. While spontaneous, trial-by-trial phasic arousal bursts have been shown to systematically suppress choice biases, it remains unknown whether a sustained, exogenously driven tonic elevation in arousal operates through an identical framework. We hypothesized that if tonic somatic noise mirrors phasic arousal dynamics, starting-point bias () would systematically shift toward structural neutrality () under high effort; alternatively, the chronic overhead of the motor task might lock in stable, energy-saving strategic heuristics.
1.6 The Present Study
The present chapter applies hierarchical Bayesian drift-diffusion modeling to examine how physical effort (Low: 5% MVC vs. High: 40% MVC isometric handgrip) modulates latent decision parameters in older adults performing auditory and visual same-different discrimination tasks. Trial-by-trial task-evoked pupil responses (TEPR) serve as an exploratory index of phasic arousal to test whether within-person arousal fluctuations predict changes in evidence accumulation or response bias. Together, these analyses test whether the characteristic slowing and cautiousness of older decision-makers reflect fixed capacity limits or physiological-state-dependent features modulated by effort-induced arousal.
2. Methods
2.1 Participants
Participants were recruited from communities surrounding the University of California, Riverside, through multiple channels including direct mail, posted flyers, social media advertisements, and word-of-mouth referrals. Eligibility criteria required participants to be aged 60–90 years, possess normal or corrected-to-normal vision and hearing, demonstrate sufficient English language comprehension to follow experimental instructions, and exhibit right-hand dominance. Exclusion criteria comprised contraindications for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), current use of psychotropic medications, clinically significant neurological disorders, and major systemic health conditions including diabetes mellitus.
Sixty-nine individuals initially enrolled in the study. Two participants were excluded following enrollment: one due to suspected mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and another due to possible essential tremor, yielding a final sample of 67 older adults (age M = 69.5 years, SD = 6.4, range = 60–87 years). Table 1 reports baseline demographics for the full sample; the extended neuropsychological battery and additional baseline descriptives are provided in Appendix A9. After applying the RT threshold and quality criteria (fewer than 100 analyzable trials in a given task), 5 participants contributed data to only one task, yielding task-specific analytic samples of N = 64 (Auditory) and N = 65 (Visual). Sample size was determined via a priori power analysis (G*Power 3.1; (Faul et al., 2007)). For a repeated-measures ANOVA with two within-subject conditions, medium effect size (), , and power = .80, a minimum of 34 participants was required. The achieved sample of  exceeds this threshold by approximately 2 and is consistent with published hierarchical DDM studies reliably detecting group-level condition effects with – (Ratcliff et al., 2004; Wiecki et al., 2013). Twelve participants performed at or near chance level ( 55% accuracy, compared to the 50% two-alternative forced-choice chance baseline) in some experimental conditions but were retained in analyses to maximize sample size, as hierarchical modeling approaches borrow statistical strength across participants to stabilize individual-level parameter estimates.
All participants provided written informed consent and received either course credit or monetary compensation for their participation. Study procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of California, Riverside, and all experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with approved guidelines and regulations.

	2.2 Stimuli and Task Design
Task overview Participants completed two response-signal same/different discrimination tasks—an Auditory Detection Task (ADT) and a Visual Detection Task (VDT)—each performed under concurrent hand-grip effort manipulation. In both tasks, a standard and probe stimulus were presented in succession, and participants judged whether the stimuli were the same or different using button presses, followed by a confidence rating.
Trial structure. Both tasks shared the same response-signal timeline (see Figure 1, Panel A). Each trial began with a jittered blank interval (1.5–4.5 s) displaying only a gray background. Participants were then shown a 3-second rectangular grip gauge with a marker positioned at either 5% or 40% from the bottom, indicating the target force level to be maintained using a hand-held dynamometer (left hand) at the corresponding percentage of their maximum voluntary contraction (MVC). MVC was determined during a brief calibration procedure prior to each task. Following the grip period, a 0.25-second blank screen and 0.5-second fixation dot preceded stimulus presentation.
Auditory Detection Task (ADT). The standard stimulus was a 0.1-second sinusoidal tone at 1000 Hz. After a 0.5-second inter-stimulus interval, the probe stimulus was presented as a 0.1-second tone that was either identical to the standard ( = 0) or positively offset by . For drift-diffusion model (DDM) analyses, the four non-zero offsets were collapsed into two difficulty bins: Hard = {8, 16} Hz and Easy = {32, 64} Hz, with Standard = 0 Hz.
Visual Detection Task (VDT). The standard stimulus was a centrally presented sinusoidal Gabor patch (spatial frequency 1.5 cycles/degree, Michelson contrast 0.20, diameter 4°, duration 0.1 s). After a 0.5-second inter-stimulus interval, the probe stimulus was presented as a 0.1-second Gabor that was either identical to the standard ( = 0) or showed a positive contrast increase of , with all other parameters held constant. For DDM analyses, offsets were collapsed into Hard = {0.06, 0.12} and Easy = {0.24, 0.48}, with Standard = 0.
Response collection. Following stimulus offset, participants relaxed their grip and had a 3-second response window to indicate whether the two stimuli were the same or different using their right hand (index finger = “different”; middle finger = “same”). This was immediately followed by another 3-second window in which participants provided a confidence rating on a 4-point scale (1 = low confidence, 4 = high confidence).
Trial counts and monitoring. Each task comprised 150 trials distributed across five blocks of 30 trials each. Within each block, the five stimulus levels (Standard plus four offsets) were crossed with the two effort conditions (5% vs. 40% MVC) and presented in pseudorandom order with equal proportions (three repetitions per stimulus × effort combination). Hand-grip performance was monitored after each block, and participants received corrective feedback if they consistently failed to maintain the dynamometer at the designated force level (near the grip gauge marker) and/or for the correct duration (until offset of the probe stimulus).
2.3 Equipment
The experimental task was programmed and executed on an Alienware laptop. Visual stimuli were presented via a PROPixx projector (VPixx Technologies, Montreal, Canada; 480 Hz refresh rate, 1920×1080 resolution) and viewed through a head coil-mounted mirror (viewing distance  12 cm). Auditory stimuli were delivered via MRI-safe earbuds (Newmatic Medical, Duluth, GA; 30 dB NRR). Behavioral responses were recorded using a RESPONSEpixx fiber-optic response box, hand-grip force via a MRI-compatible dynamometer (Current Designs Inc., Philadelphia, USA; 100 Hz sampling), and pupil diameter via an MRI-compatible TRACKPixx binocular eye tracker (VPixx Technologies, Montreal, Canada; 2000 Hz sampling). Stimuli were generated using Psychophysics Toolbox 3.0.18 (Brainard & Vision, 1997; Kleiner et al., 2007) in MATLAB R2022b (The MathWorks, Natick, MA). Data were collected inside an MRI scanner; neuroimaging results are reported elsewhere.
2.4 Data Processing and Quality Control
Reaction time definition. In this response-signal design, reaction time (RT) was initially recorded as the latency from response-screen onset (“Same or Different?” prompt) to button press, termed cue-locked RT. However, perceptual evidence becomes available at probe onset (the second stimulus). To align RT with evidence accumulation onset, we derived probe-onset-locked RT by adding the fixed post-probe delay to cue-locked RT:

where 0.35 s comprises 0.10 s probe duration plus 0.25 s grip-relaxation period. Probe-onset-locked RT served as the primary timing measure for DDM interpretation, as it aligns the decision process with evidence onset. Under this convention, the DDM non-decision time parameter () includes the fixed 0.35 s response-gating delay plus residual encoding and motor components. Cue-locked RT was retained for sensitivity analyses. This probe-onset-locked timing is our chosen convention to align  with evidence onset; some DDM studies alternatively use cue-locked or response-locked reference points depending on task structure.
Gated response-signal validity. Overt responses were withheld until the response screen (a fixed 0.35 s after probe offset), so cue-locked RT mixes pre-response processing with enforced waiting. Probe-onset locking shifts the accumulation clock to evidence onset while absorbing the fixed gating interval into  (intercept-only in the primary model). This follows standard practice for response-signal and masked-response designs: condition contrasts are identified within a shared timing regime, and absolute  estimates are not interpreted as pure motor time (Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008; Starns & Ratcliff, 2010). We treat probe-onset locking as a conservative alignment choice; free-response replications without enforced waiting would be needed to isolate encoding-only  components.
Trial exclusion criteria. To minimize the influence of premature or accidental button presses, trials with cue-locked RT < 0.20 s were excluded from the primary analysis (sensitivity analyses using alternative cutoffs of 0.15 s and 0.25 s are reported in Appendix A). This lower bound was applied to cue-locked RT before constructing probe-onset-locked RT for the primary DDM models. Additional exclusions included missed responses, trials from runs flagged for invalid performance (defined as run-level accuracy at or below chance, indicating the participant did not engage with the task), and trials with non-positive or missing RT values (i.e., recording artifacts where RT was logged as zero or a negative number). For pupil-linked DDM models, trials additionally required valid pupil data (see Pupillometry Preprocessing section).
2.5 Experimental Design and Statistical Modeling
Design. The study employed a 2 (Task: ADT, VDT) × 3 (Difficulty: Standard, Easy, Hard) × 2 (Effort: Low, High) within-subjects factorial design, yielding 12 experimental conditions per participant. Because difficulty is determined by stimulus offset magnitude and thus unknown at trial onset, it was not included as a predictor of starting-point bias in the primary DDM specification.
Modeling approach. ADT and VDT were modeled jointly in a single hierarchical Bayesian DDM, with Task (ADT vs. VDT) included as a fixed effect on starting-point bias . This joint specification estimates task-specific parameter offsets (i.e., how ADT and VDT differ in bias) while sharing subject-level random-effect variance across tasks, allowing hierarchical shrinkage to stabilize individual-level estimates. Importantly, drift rate  and boundary separation  were not modeled as functions of Task in the primary model — instead, task-specific estimates for these parameters are provided as a sensitivity analysis in Appendix A4.3. This choice reflects our primary interest in the pooled effort and difficulty effects, with task-modality differences treated as secondary context.
2.6 Statistical Analysis Overview
Analyses proceeded in three stages. First, manipulation checks confirmed that difficulty and effort affected behavioral performance (accuracy and RT) as intended, using generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMM, binomial family) for accuracy and linear mixed-effects models (LMM) for median RT via lme4 (Bates et al., 2015), each with fixed effects for Difficulty, Effort, and Task and random intercepts for participant; fixed-effects summaries used broom.mixed (Anderson et al., 2024). Full results are reported in Appendix A7. Second, the primary DDM analysis used a hierarchical Bayesian Wiener diffusion model fit via brms (Bürkner, 2017) in R (R Core Team, 2025), with model comparison via Pareto-smoothed importance sampling LOO cross-validation (PSIS-LOO; (Vehtari et al., 2017)); full model specification is described in Section 2.10. Third, pupil-linked DDM extensions tested whether trial-wise task-evoked pupil responses (TEPR) modulated drift rate or response bias, using a nested sequence of pupil-augmented models compared via PSIS-LOO (Section 2.10.1). Sensitivity analyses across RT cutoff thresholds are reported in Appendix A4.2. Exploratory individual-differences analyses examined whether LC structural MRI metrics (Appendix A8; (Bennett et al., 2024)) and neuropsychological test scores (Appendix A9) related to DDM parameters or behavioral effort costs; these analyses were not used for model selection or primary hypothesis testing.
2.7 Pupillometry Preprocessing and AUC Features
Recording and hardware. Pupil diameter was continuously recorded using an MRI-compatible TRACKPixx binocular eye tracker (VPixx Technologies, Montreal, Canada) sampling at 2000 Hz. Raw eye-tracking data were exported as .mat files and processed through a multi-stage preprocessing pipeline designed to ensure high-quality pupillometry measurements.
Artifact correction and quality control. Blink artifacts and invalid samples were removed using the ET-remove-artifacts MATLAB toolbox (Huang et al., 2020), which employs a velocity-based detection algorithm configured with a 100th-order low-pass filter (passband: 10 Hz, stopband: 12 Hz) and peak/trough threshold factors of 5 standard deviations above baseline. Artifacts exceeding 2 seconds in duration were replaced with missing values rather than interpolated, following established methodological guidelines indicating that linear reconstruction over extended durations artificially compresses natural physiological variance and introduces spurious data trends ((Kret & Sjak-Shie, 2019; Mathôt, 2018)). All recordings then underwent manual inspection using the toolbox’s interactive plot editor to identify and correct any remaining undetected artifacts.
Trial alignment and downsampling. Cleaned pupil data were trial-aligned using event timestamps synchronized with behavioral event markers, then downsampled from 2000 Hz to 250 Hz using an 8th-order anti-aliasing filter to preserve signal quality while reducing computational demands. Baseline correction was applied by subtracting the mean pupil diameter during the baseline window (-0.5 s to 0 s relative to squeeze onset) from all timepoints in the trial.
Decision-Response AUC specification and rationale. We quantified trial-wise phasic cognitive workload by calculating the baseline-corrected pupil area under the curve (AUC) via trapezoidal integration. This metric, functionally designated as the Decision-Response AUC, was extracted from a 3.0-second window anchored directly to evidence onset, spanning from 0.3 to 3.3 seconds post-probe. This placement corresponds to 4.65 to 7.65 seconds relative to the initial handgrip squeeze, capturing the transient, stimulus-locked phasic arousal fluctuation riding on top of the sustained tonic effort baseline. Delaying the window onset by 300 milliseconds directly accounts for the inherent neurophysiological lag of the pupillary efferent pathway. The 3.0-second window duration is highly specialized. It encapsulates the complete morphological profile of the transient task-evoked pupil response (TEPR), which characteristically peaks at approximately 2.95 seconds post-stimulus in this cohort. Because the explicit response prompt appeared 0.35 seconds post-probe and mean cue-locked response latencies averaged 0.6 seconds, this expansive window spans both pre-decision evidence accumulation and post-decision motor and confidence processing. We directly isolated these overlapping post-decisional dynamics by entering reaction time as a covariate in all primary downstream pupil-informed models. We also defined a Truncated Decision-Response AUC—a 1.0-second integration window from 0.3 to 1.3 s post-probe (4.65 to 5.65 s relative to handgrip onset)—as a narrower alternative that minimizes post-response contamination at the cost of capturing less of the full TEPR morphology; the same nested pupil-DDM sequence was refit on this metric as a sensitivity analysis (Section 3.3).
Supplementary Figure 18 shows the response-signal timing structure used in analyses.
Confounds and sensitivity checks. To minimize potential confounds from luminance and viewing distance, the paradigm was executed within a controlled MRI environment characterized by constant background luminance and a fixed viewing distance. Motor-related autonomic activity was addressed by including reaction time as a covariate in subsequent pupil-informed models.
2.8 DDM Overview and Parameter Definitions
The DDM framework and its four parameters (, , , ) are introduced in Section 1.2; this section describes the implementation-specific decisions for the present study.
2.9 Decision Coding and Parameterization
Response-side coding rationale. We employed response-side coding (also termed stimulus coding) where the upper boundary maps to “different” choices and the lower boundary maps to “same” choices (Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008; Wiecki et al., 2013). This configuration is mathematically required to independently isolate response-selection biases from true sensory discriminability (drift rate). Accuracy-based coding layouts (correct vs. incorrect boundaries) are structurally incapable of capturing these dynamics, as a bias parameter in an accuracy matrix merely reflects a trivial preference for being correct. Because individuals performing same–different discrimination paradigms frequently exhibit prominent response-specific heuristics (e.g., a conservative tendency to favor the “same” classification), anchoring boundaries directly to the physical response alternatives allows the model to estimate true pre-stimulus starting-point preferences. Under this parameterization, evidence accumulation directed toward a true “Standard” (same) stimulus is represented as a negative drift rate traveling toward the lower boundary, rather than a processing deficit.
Implementation and model leverage. Response-side coding was implemented by constructing a binary response vector, coded as 1 for “different” judgments and 0 for “same” judgments. Standard () trials provide critical mathematical leverage for identifying baseline starting-point bias () under this coding scheme. Because these baseline trials contain zero objective sensory evidence, they effectively isolate automatic response preferences from evidence accumulation speed. Full link function specifications and the underlying Wiener likelihood equations are detailed in Appendix A6.
2.10 Model Specification: Standard-only Calibration and Primary Model
Standard-only bias calibration model. To structurally isolate baseline starting-point bias from active drift-rate estimation, we executed a two-stage modeling pipeline. The first stage fit a targeted calibration model restricted exclusively to Standard trials ( 3,600 trials), which establishes an empirical baseline for choice preference in the complete absence of sensory evidence shifts. Starting-point bias () was allowed to vary as a function of task modality and effort condition—both of which were explicitly known to the participant pre-trial—but was constrained to be invariant across perceptual difficulty levels, as trial-wise difficulty was randomized and entirely unknown prior to evidence presentation. The full parameter estimates and descriptive levels resulting from this calibration step are detailed in Appendix A2.
Primary analysis model. Our primary behavioral DDM integrated both testing domains by modeling the Auditory Discrimination Task (ADT) and Visual Discrimination Task (VDT) jointly within a single hierarchical Bayesian framework. This joint configuration enters task modality as a fixed effect on starting-point bias (), allowing the model to isolate task-specific parameter offsets while sharing participant-level random-effect variance across sensory domains. This hierarchical design leverages partial pooling via shrinkage to stabilize individual parameter estimates and maximize statistical power for detecting our core effects of interest.
Within this joint model architecture, drift rate () and boundary separation () were estimated as additive functions of perceptual task difficulty (treatment-coded, Standard as reference) and tonic effort condition (treatment-coded, Low as reference). Starting-point bias () was modeled as a function of task and effort, but remained excluded from difficulty subformulas because trial difficulty cannot be anticipated before evidence accumulation begins (see Figure 1, Panel C for a visual summary of predictor mappings). Non-decision time () was set to an intercept-only specification based on pilot identification constraints inherent to the gated response-signal design. Random intercepts by participant were comprehensively estimated for , , and , while  was retained as a fixed population parameter to ensure strict model identifiability. Task-separated parameter profiles are provided as an explicit sensitivity check in Appendix A4.3 to validate that joint pooling did not obscure modality-specific trends.
RT timing convention. In this response-signal design, reaction time was defined as the latency from response-screen onset to the button press. However, because perceptual evidence becomes available immediately at probe onset, modeling distributions strictly from the execution cue presents a chronological misalignment between evidence availability and the mathematical integration start point. To ensure the diffusion window encompasses the entire information-processing timeline, probe-onset-locked RT was derived by adding the fixed 0.35-s post-probe delay (0.10-s probe duration plus 0.25-s grip-relaxation period) to the raw cue-locked value.
Probe-onset-locked RT served as our primary timing convention because it correctly aligns the diffusion process with evidence onset (Figure 19). Under this convention, the non-decision time parameter () absorbs the fixed 0.35-s response-gating delay alongside standard encoding and motor execution overhead (Ratcliff et al., 2007; Voss et al., 2004). While this constant addition can compress parameter variance in peripheral timing estimates, anchoring the model timeline to evidence onset is more theoretically appropriate for isolating accumulation dynamics under concurrent motor load. Raw cue-locked latency profiles and alternative lower-bound filtering cutoffs (0.15 s and 0.25 s) were retained for sensitivity analyses, which are reported in Appendix A4.
Behavioral validation checks. To confirm that our perceptual difficulty and physical effort manipulations systematically altered gross behavioral performance independent of DDM assumptions, we conducted baseline mixed-effects model checks restricted to active discrimination trials (Easy and Hard conditions). Perceptual accuracy was evaluated using a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM, binomial logistic link), and median reaction times were evaluated using a linear mixed model (LMM). Both validation frameworks entered task modality, perceptual difficulty, and physical effort as fixed effects, alongside random participant intercepts. Full model parameters and frequentist significance metrics for these baseline checks are reported transparently in Appendix A7.
2.10.1 Pupil-Linked DDM Extensions
Building upon the baseline behavioral model, we implemented hierarchical, pupil-informed regression extensions to evaluate whether within-person physiological fluctuations modulate parameters of choice on a trial-by-trial basis (Wiecki et al., 2013). The physical metric used for these extensions was the baseline-corrected Decision-Response AUC. To isolate true intra-individual variation from stable, between-participant differences in eye morphology, these trial-wise integration values were z-scored individually within each participant’s complete timecourse profile. This localized transformation yielded a standardized single-trial metric that serves as our functional physiological proxy for transient arousal fluctuations during the decision window.
To explicitly adjudicate between alternative mechanisms of state-dependent cognitive control, we evaluated a nested sequence of model configurations (de Gee et al., 2017). The baseline behavioral model excluded all trial-level physiological inputs, establishing a structural reference point where parameter variance was governed solely by categorical task conditions. A bias-only model allowed this standardized pupil metric to modulate starting-point bias () through a logistic link function, testing the hypothesis that momentary arousal suppresses pre-existing response preferences and drives the initial decision architecture toward neutrality (). Finally, a dual-dependency model allowed the pupil metric to simultaneously modulate both bias and drift rate (), testing whether momentary arousal additionally alters the efficiency of central sensory processing under a sustained motor load. We compared the relative predictive success of these architectures using Pareto-smoothed importance sampling leave-one-out cross-validation (PSIS-LOO; Vehtari et al. (2017)), focusing population-level inference on the posterior regression coefficients () estimated on the link scale.
Pupil × Perceptual Difficulty interaction model. To evaluate the classic Yerkes–Dodson prediction that the effects of arousal on decision-making diverge as a function of immediate cognitive demands—with the optimal arousal threshold shifting according to task complexity—we implemented an interaction model crossing the standardized pupil metric with perceptual task difficulty. This specification directly evaluates the integrated resource-arousal framework established in our introduction, examining whether within-person fluctuations in phasic arousal differentially modulate parameter dynamics across sensory difficulty levels. In the primary nested sequence, pupil was evaluated on starting-point bias (), drift + bias (, ), and pupil × difficulty on drift (Models 1–3). To test the Cavanagh (2014) boundary account directly, we additionally fit a Cavanagh-style extension allowing pupil to modulate boundary separation () only, compared to the same- behavioral baseline via PSIS-LOO (Decision-Response and Truncated Decision-Response AUC windows; Section 3.3, Appendix A5). Non-decision time remained intercept-only.

4. Discussion
4.1 Summary of Key Findings
Results confirmed Hypothesis 1 (H1): High effort reduced drift rate, consistent with resource competition or supra-optimal arousal (Section 3.2.1). For Hypothesis 2 (H2), the boundary-separation effort contrast was directionally positive (, 95% CrI ; ) but all posterior mass fell within the project ROPE, so we do not interpret it as meaningful strategic caution (Section 3.2.1). Hypothesis 3 (H3) could not be tested directly: the response-signal design required constraining  to an intercept-only specification for model stability, so no primary-model test of H3 was possible; an exploratory effort coefficient on  was small and highly imprecise, and no firm conclusion about peripheral slowing can be drawn from it (Section 3.2.1). For Hypothesis 4 (H4), starting-point bias showed a near-zero, uncertain effort contrast, providing no credible evidence of a shift toward neutrality under High effort (Section 3.2.2). Importantly, while High effort reduced accuracy (manipulation check: , ), it did not reliably slow reaction time ($= 0.011 s, 95% CI [-0.019, 0.041]). This dissociation — lower accuracy without RT slowing — is explained by the DDM decomposition: drift rate declined (evidence quality) while boundary separation changed only negligibly under the project ROPE. The effort → drift effect was robust to adding task on drift, though task itself modulated absolute drift (Section 3.2.1, Appendix~A4.3). Exploratory trial-wise pupil-linked models were inconclusive (Section 3.3); see also Limitations below. The overall pattern is most consistent with concurrent physical effort degrading evidence accumulation quality while producing only a negligible boundary adjustment, rather than a large strategic reconfiguration of decision thresholds or pre-stimulus bias.
4.2 Drift rate under effort
The reduction in drift rate under High effort (40% MVC) relative to Low effort is the central finding and directly supports H1. Interpreted through Adaptive Gain Theory (Aston-Jones & Cohen, 2005), this pattern is consistent with supra-optimal arousal under sustained grip: noise in neural representations degrades the signal-to-noise ratio driving evidence accumulation (Mather & Harley, 2016; Mikneviciute et al., 2022). The DDM localizes the primary cost to evidence quality (), with only a directionally positive but ROPE-negligible boundary effect and no shift in starting-point bias (Section 3.2.1). Non-decision time () could not be tested in the primary model (Section 2.10).
4.3 Boundary separation and strategic rigidity
The H2 posterior is directionally positive (, 95% CrI ; ) but 100% of the posterior mass lies within the  log-scale project ROPE, so we treat it as practically equivalent to zero rather than a compensatory caution adjustment. In natural-scale terms, the posterior median corresponds to approximately 2% of baseline boundary separation ( 2.27 in the Standard condition; Table 6) — much smaller than routine variation in  across difficulty levels ( ranging from 2.12 to 2.27). Empirical support for criterion widening under arousal comes from Cavanagh et al. (2014), who showed that pupil-indexed arousal during high-conflict choices predicted increased boundary separation — though that manipulation targeted transient cognitive conflict rather than sustained physical effort.
Notably, boundary estimates varied substantially by stimulus-driven difficulty (Easy: 2.06, Standard: 2.27, Hard: 2.12; Table 6), confirming that the model is sensitive to caution adjustments driven by bottom-up perceptual demands. Future work using free-response paradigms (without response cues) could better isolate strategic flexibility from design-imposed temporal constraints and provide a more powerful test of effort-related criterion adjustment at 40% MVC.
A potential concern with response-side coding is whether the Wiener DDM misinterprets Standard trial responses — where the correct answer is ‘Same’ (lower boundary) — as errors, because they accumulate toward a negative drift (lower boundary rather than upper). This concern does not apply in the present implementation. The decresponse argument in brms encodes the observed response (Different = 1, Same = 0) as the boundary indicator, not a correctness flag. The model observes the actual RT and response, infers the latent evidence trajectory that would produce that RT–accuracy combination given the full design, and estimates parameters that best explain the joint RT × accuracy distribution across all conditions simultaneously. Accordingly, the strong negative drift on Standard trials () does not indicate that the model treats these as error trials; it correctly captures the fact that participants accurately pressed ‘Same’ by accumulating evidence toward the lower boundary. The fact that boundary separation is higher on Standard trials () than on Hard () or Easy () further validates this interpretation: the model responds to the exhaustive search demands of Standard trials by estimating a wider, more cautious boundary — the theoretically correct direction — rather than collapsing the boundary as it would if it incorrectly coded these as high-error conditions.
Boundary separation vs. starting-point bias: Different timescales of adjustment. It is important to clarify an apparent asymmetry in the modeling decisions. Starting-point bias  was constrained to be constant across difficulty levels, reflecting the logical impossibility of pre-trial criterion adjustment when trial difficulty is randomized and unknown prior to stimulus onset. In contrast, boundary separation  was estimated as a function of difficulty and varied systematically (Standard:  = 2.27; Hard:  = 2.12; Easy:  = 2.06; Table 6).
This is not a contradiction. The two parameters reflect adjustments occurring at different timescales. Starting-point bias  represents a true pre-stimulus strategic choice — where participants position themselves before evidence accumulation begins — and can only be set based on information available before the trial (task modality, effort condition). Boundary separation , by contrast, is not a pre-trial strategic decision but rather an effective decision threshold inferred by the model from the shape of the empirical RT distribution within each condition. Participants do not “set” this boundary in advance; rather, the model recovers it as the latent parameter that best explains the observed RT variance under diffusion process assumptions (Ratcliff, 1985; Voss et al., 2004). Over many trials, difficulty-dependent differences in RT dispersion and accuracy produce systematically different  estimates even though individual participants are unaware of upcoming difficulty.
The larger  on Standard trials reflects the model’s accommodation of the characteristically broader, more right-skewed RT distribution observed on those trials — a distributional signature of the exhaustive comparison process required to confirm perfect sameness (Farell, 1985; Voss et al., 2004). In this sense,  captures the mechanistic consequence of within-trial processing demands, while  captures a pre-trial strategic bias. Both are legitimate sources of condition-specific variation, but only  requires advance knowledge of the trial type.
In interpreting the TEPR as a relative index of phasic arousal, we acknowledge measurement constraints under sustained grip (Section 3.3, Table 20).
4.4 Starting-point bias and pupil-linked arousal
The near-zero effort contrast on starting-point bias (H4; , 95% CrI ; ) provides no credible evidence that concurrent handgrip at 40% MVC shifted pre-existing response preferences in older adults — though the credible interval does not formally exclude a small effect, and no equivalence threshold was established. A key interpretive consideration is the distinction between tonic and phasic arousal: the de Gee et al. (2020) bias-resetting effect was demonstrated using phasic, within-trial arousal fluctuations indexed by pupil dilation, whereas the present H4 test relied on a sustained tonic effort manipulation. Tonic arousal elevation may not produce the same criterion-resetting dynamics as a phasic arousal event — a distinction that future work pairing trial-level TEPR with starting-point bias estimates could address directly. In the standard-only calibration model, starting-point bias was consistently above 0.5 (, 95% CrI [0.51, 0.64]; Table 7), indicating a latent tendency to initiate accumulation closer to the “Different” boundary.
This latent starting-point offset should not be read directly as a liberal behavioral criterion in signal-detection terms. Manipulation checks showed near-ceiling accuracy on Standard (same) trials but poor accuracy on Hard trials (~30%; Appendix A7) — a pattern dominated by misses (failure to report “different” when stimuli differed), not false alarms on same trials. The strongly negative drift on Standard trials (2 (Low effort) to −1.17 (High effort) on Standard trials) and the joint estimation of  and  across difficulty levels mean that a  starting point coexists with accurate “Same” responding rather than excess “Different” responses. The offset is therefore better interpreted as a DDM starting-point parameter under response-side coding — potentially reflecting a “Different-as-default” processing heuristic (Farell, 1985; Nosofsky & Palmeri, 1997) — than as a liberal SDT criterion producing elevated false-alarm rates.
Hard-trial accuracy at or below chance for many participants also indicates that some Hard offsets sat near perceptual threshold, weakening the assumption of a clean three-level difficulty gradient; this is noted in Limitations below. The absence of a credible effort contrast on  is consistent with a stable task strategy rather than momentary arousal-driven criterion resetting, though this remains a graded null.
The absence of a detectable phasic bias-resetting effect stands in contrast to prior work in younger adults (de Gee et al., 2020) and may reflect age-related attenuation of phasic LC-NE signals (Huang & Clewett, 2024). Exploratory trial-wise pupil-linked models were inconclusive (Section 3.3); see also Limitations below. In the primary model, VDT showed higher starting-point bias toward “different” than ADT (, 95% CrI ; ; logit task offset), whereas the standard-only calibration model shows the opposite ordering (, 95% CrI ; ), illustrating the – trade-off when drift is not held constant across difficulty. Exploratory LC-MRI correlates (Appendix A8) did not link LC integrity to task-specific bias or to the group-level effort effect on drift, though those tests were underpowered.
4.5 Temporal dynamics across trials
Exploratory choice-history models (Appendix A10.1) showed a small but reliable perseveration tendency: pre-stimulus bias shifted by approximately 0.065 logit units toward the previous response, with the 95% CrI excluding zero. High effort (40% MVC) did not amplify this effect (previous-trial response × effort interaction  = −0.001, 95% CrI [−0.065, 0.064]). Together with the ROPE-negligible H2 boundary effect and the H4 bias null, this pattern suggests that effort degrades evidence quality upstream while producing only limited downstream adjustment in criterion-setting or history-driven responding (Fischer & Whitney, 2014; Wickens, 2008).
Within-session fatigue models (Appendix A10.2) showed a complementary pattern. Boundary separation relaxed slightly from the first to the second half of each run (more pronounced under Low effort), but drift rate did not decline credibly within runs, and age did not moderate the trajectory. The primary within-run dynamic is modest criterion adjustment, not accumulation-stage exhaustion (Drugowitsch et al., 2012; Tajima et al., 2016). Effort’s between-condition effect remains localized to drift rate (), not to the temporal dynamics of boundary separation under load.
4.6 Modeling constraints
H3 could not be tested in the primary model because the response-signal design required an intercept-only  specification (Section 2.10). An exploratory effort effect on  was small and imprecise ( = 0.02, 95% CrI [−0.05, 0.09]), precluding firm conclusions about peripheral slowing. Accuracy declines under High effort align more directly with reduced drift rate than with shifts in  (Table 21).
4.7 Limitations and Future Directions
Several limitations constrain the interpretation of these findings. First, the standard Wiener DDM assumes constant drift within each trial and does not include across-trial variability in drift (s_v), starting point (s_z), or non-decision time (s_t0). Second, the response-signal design enforced a fixed delay before overt responding; probe-onset RT alignment and an intercept-only t_0 identify condition effects within that shared timing regime but do not support strong claims about pure encoding or motor t_0 in isolation (Section 2.10). Reaction times are measured from response-screen onset rather than stimulus onset, confining the interpretation of t_0 to motor execution and response selection rather than early perceptual encoding. These design choices were necessary given the MRI-compatible grip-force paradigm but limit generalizability to free-response paradigms where t_0 reflects the full encoding-decision-motor chain. Posterior predictive checks flagged all 12 experimental cells against pre-specified KS/QP thresholds (Table 10), with deviations concentrated in fast RT tails (worst in Easy/VDT). Mid-body quantile–probability alignment was nevertheless acceptable (Appendix A3, Table 11), and key effort contrasts were stable across alternative RT lower-bound thresholds (Appendix A4.2, Table 14). Future extensions incorporating across-trial variability, a fast-guess contaminant mixture, or urgency-collapsing boundaries may improve absolute fit (Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008).

Third, the primary trial-wise pupil predictor—the Decision-Response AUC—spans both pre- and post-response epochs (Section 3.3). We repeated the nested pupil-DDM comparisons using the Truncated Decision-Response AUC (0.3–1.3 s post-probe; 12,150 trials with valid truncated TEPR) and Cavanagh-style pupil→a extensions on both windows; conclusions were unchanged (|ΔELPD| < 4; near-zero or small coefficients without LOO advantage). Remaining ambiguity likely reflects concurrent motor–autonomic confounds under sustained grip rather than window choice alone.

Fourth, Hard-trial accuracy fell near or below chance for many participants (Appendix A7), indicating that some Hard offsets sat near perceptual threshold rather than forming a clean graded difficulty manipulation above chance. Parameter estimates for Hard conditions should be interpreted with this floor effect in mind. Complementary sensitivity analyses for task-on-drift pooling and related robustness checks are reported in Appendix A4 (Table 16, Table 14) where already completed; a Standard+Easy-only primary refit excluding Hard trials remains planned.

Fifth, the concurrent isometric handgrip manipulation (40% MVC) may interact with motor execution in ways not fully captured by small fixed effects on t_0; future work integrating EMG or kinematic measures could disentangle central resource competition from peripheral motor interference. Linear Ballistic Accumulator or race models may also better accommodate the fast-tail RT dynamics observed in Easy-difficulty conditions, and future work could explore whether these model families yield the same substantive pattern of effort and difficulty effects on decision parameters.

Sixth, exploratory LC-MRI and neuropsychological correlates (Appendices A8 and A9) were underpowered for individual-differences moderation and did not enter the primary inferential framework; they are reported as descriptive context for sample characterization and do not alter the group-level DDM conclusions below.

4.8 Conclusions
Concurrent 40% MVC isometric grip degraded perceptual evidence accumulation in older adults more than it reorganized decision thresholds or pre-response bias. The hierarchical Wiener DDM localized the primary cost to drift rate  — the signal-to-noise ratio of evidence integration — with a credible interval excluding zero. The inferential hierarchy was as follows.
First and strongest: drift rate was reliably lower under High than Low effort (small relative to difficulty contrasts, but directionally certain). Second: boundary separation was directionally higher under High effort but practically negligible under the project ROPE, whereas starting-point bias did not shift credibly — inconsistent with large strategic reconfiguration. Exploratory choice-history models showed small perseveration in bias without effort amplification; within-run fatigue affected boundary separation more than drift.
Third and most uncertain: non-decision time could not be tested in the primary model because the response-signal design required an intercept-only . The exploratory log- effort term was small and imprecise. Pupil-linked mechanisms were inconclusive (Section 3.3).
Exploratory individual-differences analyses in the same cohort did not alter this group-level story. LC-MRI correlates (Appendix A8; (Bennett et al., 2024)) were more closely related to between-person variability in sensitivity and baseline boundary settings than to mean perceptual sensitivity, trial-wise TEPR, or the effort effect on drift. Extended neuropsychological characterization (Appendix A9) did not indicate that global cognition explained the effort → drift pattern. Taken together, under concurrent physical load, older adults mainly accumulate less evidence per unit time, with only limited adjustment in caution or bias. Exploratory LC and neuropsychological appendices contextualize individual differences but do not change the group-level account.

A6.1 Response-Signal Timing Structure
  

Figure 18: Structural schematic of a single trial timeline and task-evoked pupil response (TEPR) integration windows. The full timeline is anchored to initial somatic contraction (Squeeze Onset at 0.00 s). Perceptual evidence becomes available at Probe Onset (4.35 s), followed by the execution prompt (Response Prompt at 4.70 s). Phasic arousal features are extracted using two baseline-corrected windows opening at 4.65 s (probe onset + 300 ms neurophysiological lag). The primary Decision-Response AUC window spans a full 3.0 seconds to capture the complete late-peaking morphology of the older cohort’s pupillary response, intentionally encompassing both choice formation and post-decisional processing. The Truncated Decision-Response AUC window spans 1.0 second (0.3–1.3 s post-probe), evaluated in a sensitivity refit of the nested pupil-DDM models (Section 3.3).
	Primary analyses used probe-onset-locked RT. Figure 19 shows the ~0.35 s shift from cue-locked RT. Cue-locked RT is used only in sensitivity checks (Appendix A4.2).
  

Figure 19: RT Timing Convention Validation. Histograms showing the distribution of response times for cue-locked (measured from response-window onset) and probe-onset-locked (measured from probe stimulus onset) RT definitions across all trials. The probe-onset distribution is shifted rightward by approximately 0.35 s, corresponding to the fixed post-probe delay before the response window opens. This shift validates correct implementation of the timing transformation described in Methods. All primary analyses use probe-onset-locked RT.

bf(rt | dec(dec_upper) ~ difficulty_level + effort_condition +

   ndt ~ 1,
   bias ~ task + effort_condition + (1 | subject_id),
   family = wiener(link_bs = "log",
                   link_ndt = "log",
                   link_bias = "logit"))
where dec(dec_upper) encodes the response boundary (1 = different, 0 = same), bs is the log-link parameter for boundary separation , ndt is the log-link parameter for non-decision time , and bias is the logit-link parameter for starting-point .
A6.4 Prior Specification
All priors were weakly informative and specified on the link scale. For intercepts, drift rate () used Normal(0, 1); boundary separation () used Normal(log(1.3), 0.25), corresponding to  on the natural scale; non-decision time () used Normal(log(0.35), 0.15), corresponding to  s on the natural scale (probe-onset-locked timing; the cue-locked variant used Normal(log(0.10), 0.15)); and starting-point bias () used Normal(0, 0.5), corresponding to  (no bias) on the probability scale. For slope parameters, drift rate slopes used Normal(0, 0.6–0.7), boundary separation slopes used Normal(0, 0.25–0.30), and bias slopes used Normal(0, 0.35). Random effects were assigned Student-t(3, 0, 0.30) priors for standard deviations and LKJ(2) priors for correlations. Prior vs. posterior for : Figure 5 (A1).

A10. Exploratory Temporal Dynamics
These models evaluate choice history and within-run fatigue. They are reported here because they support interpretation of the main effort effects but do not directly test H1-H4 or the pupil-linked analysis.
A10.1 Choice-history models
We fit two hierarchical Wiener DDM variants to test whether high somatic load increased reliance on recent responses (N = 17,199 trials after block-boundary exclusions; see Supplementary Methods). Both models added the previous response (coded ±0.5 for “Different”/“Same”) to the starting-point bias subformula. The history model tested perseveration and its modulation by effort. The win-stay/lose-shift (WSLS) model also included previous-trial accuracy, asking whether carry-over differed after correct versus incorrect responses. The history model met the pre-specified  criterion (max  = 1.003). The WSLS variant had a slightly elevated maximum  (1.014). Neither model had divergent transitions.
Baseline perseveration. Older adults showed a small but reliable tendency to repeat their previous response. In the history model, the previous-trial response coefficient on bias was positive with its 95% CrI excluding zero ( = 0.065, 95% CrI [0.005, 0.125], P( > 0) = 98.2%). On the logit scale, this corresponds to approximately a 1.5 percentage-point shift in the probability of choosing “Different” following a “Different” response. The same direction and magnitude emerged in the WSLS model (0.137 [0.017, 0.256]).
Effort does not amplify heuristic reliance. The previous-trial response × effort interaction was near zero in both models: history model  = −0.001 (95% CrI [−0.065, 0.064], P( > 0) = 48.8%); WSLS model −0.001 ([−0.065, 0.064]). Perseveration did not differ between High effort (40% MVC) and Low effort (5% MVC).
Win-stay/lose-shift dynamics. In the WSLS model, the interaction between previous-trial response and previous-trial accuracy was  = −0.107 (95% CrI [−0.225, 0.009], P( > 0) = 3.6%). The CrI just includes zero. The direction indicates that the perseveration effect was larger after incorrect trials than after correct ones. Neither the main effect of previous accuracy on bias ( ≈ 0, CrI [−0.060, 0.059]) nor the effort contrast differed from zero.
History effect on drift rate. Previous-trial response also predicted current-trial drift rate in both models ( = 0.054 [0.008, 0.100], P( > 0) = 99.1%). Drift was slightly higher when the current response direction matched the previous response.
The main estimates from both models are shown in Figure 25 (Panel C).
A10.2 Within-session fatigue trajectory
We also tested whether processing efficiency or caution changed within a run. A supplementary hierarchical Wiener DDM (N = 17,834 trials, 67 participants) included a within-run temporal factor. Each 30-trial run was split at the median trial position within each participant × task × run combination, producing first-half and second-half labels (maximum 1-trial imbalance across runs). The drift-rate subformula included effort, run half, continuous age (z-scored; 1 unit ≈ 6.4 years), and their three-way interaction. Boundary separation included effort, run half, and their interaction, with age as a main effect. The model converged satisfactorily (max  = 1.004; 0 divergent transitions).
Within-run boundary relaxation. The only parameter contrast with a 95% CrI excluding zero was the second-half change in boundary separation:  = −0.022 (95% CrI [−0.040, −0.004], P( < 0) = 99.3%). On the exponentiated scale, this corresponds to a drop from about 2.04 to 1.99 across run halves under Low effort (5% MVC). The effort × run-half interaction was positive but non-credible ( = +0.011, 95% CrI [−0.014, 0.037]). Under High effort (40% MVC), the expected boundary in the second half (≈ 2.03) was close to the first-half value. Boundary trajectories for all four condition cells are shown in Figure 25 (Panel A).
No credible drift-rate decline. The effort × run-half interaction on drift was negative but spanned zero:  = −0.035 (95% CrI [−0.100, 0.032], P( < 0) = 85.3%). Drift did not credibly decay in the second half of a run under High effort. The effort main effect on drift was directionally consistent with the primary model ( = −0.038, 95% CrI [−0.085, 0.008], P( < 0) = 94.8%), but it was non-credible in this expanded model.
Age does not moderate the trajectory. The three-way interaction (effort × run half × age on drift) was  = −0.023 (95% CrI [−0.091, 0.044], P( < 0) = 74.0%). The CrI spans zero, so there is no clear age gradient in within-run drift dynamics.
Secondary pattern. The run-half × age interaction on drift was positive and 95.8% probable ( = +0.048, 95% CrI [−0.006, 0.102]), with the CrI just including zero. Older-within-cohort participants tended to show slightly higher drift in the second half under Low effort. This pattern is exploratory.
The fatigue coefficients are plotted in Figure 25 (Panel B).
